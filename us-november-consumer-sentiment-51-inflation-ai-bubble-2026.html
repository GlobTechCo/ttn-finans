U.S. Consumer Sentiment Rises to 51.0, but Cost Pressures Persist

The University of Michigan's preliminary November index rose three points, as inflation expectations eased despite lingering cost concerns.

TTN Research DeskNov 7, 2026 · 08:59 UTC

U.S. consumer sentiment improved in early November, although households remained deeply dissatisfied with living costs. The University of Michigan's preliminary index rose to 51.0 from 48.0 in October, a 6.3% increase, according to figures released November 6. The result exceeded the 48.0 reading economists surveyed by Reuters had expected.

The improvement did not indicate a broad return of confidence. Sentiment was unchanged from November 2025, and the survey recorded a sharp divide between households with different levels of financial wealth. Consumers continued to cite high prices as their main economic concern.

Inflation expectations ease

Expected inflation over the next year declined to 4.3% from 4.7% in October. Expectations for the following five years slipped to 3.4% from 3.5%. Both remained elevated compared with the university's 2024 readings, especially the longer-term measure, which ranged from 2.8% to 3.2% that year.

The distinction between falling inflation expectations and falling prices matters. Even if consumers anticipate slower future price increases, they still face the cumulative rise in everyday costs. About 57% of respondents spontaneously said higher prices had eroded their standard of living, the highest share since May and up from 46% in February.

The latest figures therefore describe a less negative outlook, not an end to household financial pressure. The university's index of current conditions increased to 51.0 from 46.7, while its expectations index rose to 50.9 from 48.8. Both improved, but neither pointed to strong consumer confidence.

For context, TTN Finance's guide to what consumer sentiment measures explains why survey responses cannot be treated as direct evidence of changes in spending.

A split between wealth groups

Survey director Joanne Hsu said the improvement was concentrated primarily among political independents, with gains among lower- and middle-wealth households. Sentiment among respondents holding the largest stock portfolios, by contrast, fell 5%.

Hsu also reported growing concern about a possible artificial intelligence investment bubble. Those concerns appeared in respondents' comments, but the university made clear that cost-of-living complaints were much more prevalent.

The divergence is relevant to the stock market. Households with substantial investment portfolios can react to falling share prices differently from families whose finances depend principally on wages. But the 5% decline in sentiment among large stockholders is a survey result; it is not a measure of portfolio losses or consumer spending.

High capital expenditure in AI infrastructure has intensified scrutiny of the returns technology companies can earn on their investments. Our earlier reporting on power, financing and delivery risks in AI data centers examines that investment issue separately. The Michigan survey shows it has entered consumer perceptions; it does not establish whether AI equities are overvalued.

The election was outside the survey window

Interviews ran from October 20 through November 2, before the November 3 U.S. midterm elections. The initial results also preceded the sharp technology-stock selloff later in the week. Analysts cannot attribute the latest responses to those events without additional evidence.

The final November release, scheduled for November 20, will include subsequent interviews and may capture changes in perceptions after the election and market turbulence. That timing is especially important when comparing economic confidence across political groups.

The survey arrived as other indicators painted a mixed economic picture. The October employment report showed 165,000 additional jobs and unemployment at 4.1%, suggesting labor income remained a source of support. TTN Finance reviewed the figures and their revisions in its October U.S. jobs report.

Still, stronger employment figures do not resolve households' concerns about the cost of groceries, housing or other essentials. Employment data and consumer surveys answer different questions, and their signals can diverge for extended periods.

What matters next

The Federal Reserve monitors inflation expectations alongside actual inflation, wages, employment and financial conditions. One preliminary survey is insufficient to determine the timing of its next rate decision. The move from 4.7% to 4.3% in one-year expectations is relevant, but long-run expectations and incoming price data are more useful for assessing persistence.

The November 20 final reading will test whether the early improvement endured. A further decline in inflation expectations would support evidence of moderating price fears, while a reversal could reinforce concern about household purchasing power.

For now, the data support a limited conclusion: Americans became somewhat less pessimistic in early November, but high living costs remained dominant, and worries about technology valuations affected wealthier respondents. Consumer confidence improved from a weak starting point; it did not return to normal.
