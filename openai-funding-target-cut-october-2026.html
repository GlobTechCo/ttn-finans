OpenAI Reduces Fundraising Target to $20 Billion, FT Reports

OpenAI has reportedly lowered its fundraising goal to about $20 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation. The terms remain unconfirmed by the company.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-31

OpenAI has reduced the target for its latest fundraising round by $10 billion to approximately $20 billion, according to an October 30 report by the Financial Times. The newspaper said the company was seeking a valuation of about $1.25 trillion, compared with an earlier target of $1.4 trillion. Reuters subsequently reported the terms, citing the Financial Times. These are reported financing plans, not a completed transaction or an announcement of final proceeds by OpenAI.

The proposed round is smaller, but still substantial

The target reduction represents a one-third decrease in the amount OpenAI had sought to raise. The reported change in valuation is about $150 billion, or approximately 11% of the earlier $1.4 trillion figure. Investors were said to have reduced their intended commitments as technology stocks sold off and questions intensified about the cost of the industry's AI infrastructure buildout.

The numbers need careful attribution. A fundraising target is not the same thing as money received, and a proposed valuation is not a price established by public trading. Private financing terms can change before closing, or be accompanied by conditions that make headline valuations difficult to compare. OpenAI did not publicly confirm the revised terms in the reports reviewed for this article. Describing the $20 billion as an amount already raised would therefore overstate what was known.

The Financial Times also reported that the first closing could be smaller than the revised target, with additional commitments or later offerings contributing to the eventual total. Prospective investors were still negotiating allocations. Neither the final roster of backers nor the closing amount should be treated as settled.

Why investor appetite matters

AI research companies are spending heavily on computing infrastructure, engineering talent and product development. The businesses building advanced models hope that revenue from subscriptions, enterprise services and application programming interfaces will justify those commitments. Investors must assess both the size of the opportunity and the time required to turn it into durable operating cash flow.

A reduced fundraising target does not itself demonstrate that OpenAI's products have lost demand. It may reflect investors' portfolio limits, liquidity needs, changing valuations across technology markets or concerns about concentration in a small number of AI companies. Those explanations are plausible, but the reporting does not establish a single cause. A credible news account must separate the observed reduction in the proposed round from speculation about why each investor made its decision.

Financial Times reporting also placed the proposed financing against the possibility of a public listing by rival Anthropic. Competition for investors can influence timing and commitments, but the date and terms of any future listing remain uncertain. An anticipated IPO should not be presented as if regulators had approved an offering or shares were already available to purchase.

The cash question extends beyond OpenAI

The wider technology sell-off on October 30 highlighted investor scrutiny of AI-related capital expenditure. Intel shares slumped after disappointing guidance, while Amazon reported strong AWS growth alongside a substantial outflow of free cash flow linked mainly to investments in property and equipment. The contrast is significant: strong underlying demand does not guarantee near-term financial returns for every participant in the AI supply chain.

OpenAI's reported fundraising change is part of that financing discussion, though it should not be read as direct proof that investors expect the company's operating performance to worsen. The amounts are large enough to affect how private investors allocate capital to competing technologies and businesses. For additional context, TTN Finance has examined the economics of enterprise AI agents and AI-related revenue guidance from Capgemini.

What remains unconfirmed

The final size of the round, its closing date, the allocations of individual investors and the precise valuation terms remain subject to confirmation. Reuters attributed the reported change to the Financial Times rather than an independent corporate announcement. OpenAI remains privately held and has no publicly traded stock ticker. Adding a fictional OpenAI symbol or a TradingView price chart would be misleading.

The most important next evidence will be a confirmed financing announcement, any company disclosures about the structure of the round, and later information showing how investment in computing capacity relates to sales, margins and cash requirements. Until then, the central news is a reported change in the scale of a proposed private financing, not a verdict on the future of artificial intelligence.

The distinction between the size of a funding round and a company's overall valuation is fundamental. A lower fundraising target means the company seeks to receive less new capital in that transaction. It does not, by itself, demonstrate a comparable decline in product demand, revenue, or the value of existing investors' shares. The proposed valuation is a negotiated reference point and remains uncertain until the financing terms are finalized.

Readers should also distinguish company announcements from reporting based on people involved in private fundraising discussions. Neither the final subscription amounts nor any completed closing can be inferred from a reported target. Future statements from OpenAI or participating investors, if released, would provide stronger evidence of the actual financing outcome.
