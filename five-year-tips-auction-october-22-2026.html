Treasury’s $26 billion TIPS auction locks in a 2.367% real yield

A new five-year inflation-protected Treasury pays a 2.25% coupon. Its auction yield shows why inflation protection and price stability differ.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-22

The U.S. Treasury sold $26 billion of new five-year inflation-protected notes on October 22 at a real yield of 2.367%. The result establishes the return above the securities’ inflation adjustment for buyers at the auction price, giving investors a fresh benchmark for the cost of protecting purchasing power over the next five years.

The issue carries a 2.25% annual coupon and matures on October 15, 2031, according to the official auction results. Settlement is October 30. The difference between the coupon and the auction yield is important: the coupon determines interest payments, while the yield also reflects what buyers pay for the security.

Demand cleared slightly above the market’s indication

The bid-to-cover ratio was 2.41, meaning submitted bids totaled about 2.41 times the amount awarded. That measures the volume of bidding relative to supply; it does not tell investors that every bidder was willing to pay the same price, or that the security will appreciate after settlement.

Specialist publication Tipswatch reported a pre-auction when-issued yield of 2.360%. The final 2.367% result was therefore 0.7 basis point higher, a small auction tail. Buyers received a slightly higher yield than that indication suggested. This is a useful execution detail, rather than evidence on its own of a broad loss of confidence in Treasury debt.

Treasury reported a settlement index ratio of 1.00154, which links the adjusted and unadjusted principal values for this issue. It is a settlement calculation, not an additional annual interest rate. The unadjusted auction price was $99.454587 per $100 of principal. Treasury also reported an inflation-adjusted price of $99.607747 and adjusted accrued interest of $0.92863 per $1,000. Those separate figures explain why multiplying the quoted unadjusted price by an order’s face value does not necessarily reproduce the final settlement payment.

What the real yield actually promises

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, or TIPS, adjust principal with changes in the Consumer Price Index. Their fixed coupon rate is applied to that adjusted principal, with interest paid twice a year. As principal changes, the dollar amount of interest changes too. The coupon percentage itself stays fixed for the life of the note.

For illustration, a 2.25% coupon on $1,000 of principal corresponds to $22.50 of annual interest before any inflation adjustment. If adjusted principal rises, the same coupon produces a larger dollar payment. This example explains the mechanism; it is not a calculation of this auction buyer’s total return, which also depends on purchase price and subsequent inflation.

At maturity, Treasury pays the greater of original principal or inflation-adjusted principal. That protection applies at maturity. Someone who sells earlier can receive less than the purchase price if market real yields have risen. The familiar inverse relationship between bond prices and yields still matters for an inflation-linked bond.

A benchmark, not a forecast for household inflation

The 2.367% figure should not be read as Treasury’s prediction for inflation. It is a real yield, distinct from a nominal Treasury yield that does not adjust principal. Comparing the two can help assess market pricing of inflation compensation, but that comparison also reflects liquidity and risk premiums. It cannot isolate a pure forecast of future consumer prices.

Nor does the inflation adjustment track every household’s budget. A renter facing a steep renewal increase and a homeowner with a fixed mortgage can experience very different changes in living costs. The security follows its designated index, rather than an investor’s personal spending basket. That distinction matters when evaluating purchasing-power protection.

The auction follows this week’s 20-year Treasury sale, but the two instruments answer different questions: long-term nominal financing versus a shorter inflation-linked commitment. For investors assessing the new issue, the next relevant evidence will be its secondary-market real yield and the inflation adjustments over its life, rather than the coupon number viewed in isolation.
