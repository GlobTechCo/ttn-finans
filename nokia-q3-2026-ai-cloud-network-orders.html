Nokia Books €1.5 Billion of AI and Cloud Orders as Cash Demands Rise

AI demand lifts Nokia’s networking business, while accelerated restructuring and working-capital needs complicate the profit picture.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-22

Nokia secured €1.5 billion of new orders from AI and cloud customers in the third quarter, according to its interim report released Thursday. Sales to that customer group more than doubled from a year earlier. The figures show demand for the connections that move data between computing systems, even as investment in AI is more commonly associated with processors and data-center buildings.

The company’s group net sales reached €5.154 billion, up 10% as reported and 9% on a constant-currency basis. Those growth rates answer different questions: reported sales include exchange-rate effects, while the adjusted comparison helps assess the operating movement. Neither figure should be substituted for the other without specifying the basis.

Networking connects the AI buildout

Nokia sells optical and internet-protocol networking technology used to connect infrastructure. Its network-infrastructure segment reported 15% constant-currency sales growth in the quarter. Stronger demand from AI and cloud customers provides evidence that infrastructure investment is reaching connectivity suppliers, rather than stopping at the companies that design computing chips.

An AI system has to move information as well as process it. Links between equipment, buildings and regions can become constraints when computing capacity expands. More processors do not by themselves guarantee that data can reach the right place quickly enough or that a growing facility can connect efficiently with the rest of an operator’s network.

That creates an opportunity for suppliers of optical transport and routing equipment. It also means the value of their products depends on a customer’s broader deployment schedule. Power availability, equipment installation and acceptance testing can influence when an order becomes revenue. A healthy order book is commercially meaningful, but it is not the same thing as cash already received.

Comparable profit and reported profit diverge

Nokia reported comparable operating profit of €605 million, up 35%, while reported operating profit fell 22% to €196 million. Restructuring and integration costs explain much of the divergence. The company kept its full-year comparable operating-profit outlook at €2.1 billion to €2.6 billion.

The two measures should be read together. A comparable figure can make the performance of ongoing operations easier to assess by removing specified items. The reported figure still records costs borne by the company. Excluding a cost from an analytical measure does not mean the expense disappears or that it can have no effect on shareholder returns.

This is particularly relevant when a business is changing its portfolio and integrating an acquisition. Savings may emerge over time, while the work needed to achieve them affects current accounts. Investors need to inspect the reconciliation and the timing of payments, rather than assume an improving adjusted measure provides a complete picture of the transition.

Growth also absorbs cash

Nokia’s full-year assumptions now include an additional €400 million investment in working capital and a lower free-cash-flow conversion range of 35% to 55%. It expects restructuring and associated charges of €1.1 billion. These assumptions qualify the demand story: preparing to supply more equipment can require funding before the related customer payments arrive.

Working capital includes resources tied up in items such as inventory and receivables. A supplier may need components on hand to meet delivery schedules, then wait for payment after shipping equipment. That can make revenue growth and cash generation move in different directions during an expansion. It is a financing requirement that deserves attention alongside margins.

TTN’s explanation of AI supply bottlenecks describes why a hardware expansion depends on several linked inputs. Nokia’s report adds a supplier-level example: orders can expand while preparations to fulfill them consume cash. The figures do not establish the return customers will ultimately earn from the AI capacity being connected.

The next test is conversion into deliveries and cash

The next useful comparisons will track how the order book turns into revenue, whether restructuring delivers the expected operating benefits and how working capital evolves as shipments increase. Those measures would help distinguish a sustained improvement in the business from a strong quarter supported by the timing of orders or deliveries.

Nokia’s U.S. depositary receipts trade under NOK, while this earnings report uses euros. For readers following the listed security, the central question is how demand, implementation costs and cash collection work together. Thursday’s results support the case for growing AI-networking demand while making clear that supplying that growth also carries financial demands.
