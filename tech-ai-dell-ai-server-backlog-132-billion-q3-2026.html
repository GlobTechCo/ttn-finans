Dell's $132.3B AI Backlog Tests the Server Boom

Dell's record orders lifted its outlook, but converting the AI backlog into revenue and cash will test execution.

TTN Research DeskNov 25, 2026 · 3:25 AM EST

Dell Technologies’ fiscal third-quarter results show how strongly artificial-intelligence infrastructure is reshaping the company’s business. Revenue more than doubled, AI-optimized server sales nearly quadrupled and the order backlog reached a record $132.3 billion. Dell also raised its full-year revenue and AI-server forecasts. Yet the backlog is not cash already collected or revenue already recognized. The central question for investors is whether customers can finance, install and use the systems they have ordered while Dell protects the margins and cash generation that made the quarter so strong.

For the quarter ended October 30, Dell reported revenue of $54.937 billion, up 103% from a year earlier. GAAP diluted earnings per share were $8.82, compared with non-GAAP diluted EPS of $9.45. Net income reached $5.673 billion and operating cash flow was $4.459 billion. The GAAP and adjusted earnings figures use different calculation bases and should not be treated as interchangeable. Dell’s filing provides the detailed statements and reconciliations behind the results.

Infrastructure now drives the growth mix

The Infrastructure Solutions Group, which includes servers, networking and storage, generated $41.974 billion in revenue, up 198% year over year. AI-optimized server revenue rose 291% to $22.031 billion. Traditional servers and networking increased 235% to $15.013 billion, while storage revenue advanced 24% to $4.930 billion. Segment operating income reached $7.118 billion, up 308%, and the operating margin increased to 17.0% from 12.4%. The expansion therefore extends beyond accelerators: customers are also buying the networking and storage equipment needed to run larger systems.

A data centre requires memory, networking, storage, power distribution, cooling and suitable facilities as well as processors. A delay in any one component can postpone a project or limit the number of systems a customer can bring online. Dell’s infrastructure business benefits from the wider build-out, but it is exposed to the cost and availability of interdependent inputs. TTN’s analysis of AI data-centre electricity demand and its guide to semiconductor supply constraints examine two of those dependencies.

The backlog is a pipeline, not a guarantee

Dell booked $60.2 billion in AI-related orders during the quarter and ended it with a $132.3 billion AI backlog. Management said more than 7,500 customers were deploying Dell AI Factory offerings, which combine computing, storage, networking and software. Chief Operating Officer Jeff Clarke said the company screens orders for customer quality and access to capital, land and power. Those qualifications matter. A backlog records orders awaiting delivery or recognition; it does not mean the full amount will become revenue immediately or that every project will proceed on its original timetable.

Customers must still secure financing, complete data-centre capacity, obtain electricity and integrate systems into workloads that justify the expense. A project can be delayed if a site is not ready or a customer changes its spending plan. Dell’s emphasis on credit quality and access to land and power signals that management views the gap between an order and a deliverable project as an operating risk. Conversion will determine how quickly demand appears in sales and cash receipts.

Higher guidance raises the execution bar

Dell lifted its fiscal 2027 revenue outlook to $202 billion from $192 billion, implying year-over-year growth of 78%. Its forecast for AI-optimized server revenue increased to $78 billion from $74 billion. The company also raised its full-year non-GAAP diluted EPS outlook to $29.68 from $25.50. For the fourth quarter, Dell forecast revenue of $56.3 billion and non-GAAP diluted EPS of $8.40. These are management forecasts, not reported results. They set a demanding test: ship more systems while managing components, customer installation schedules and the costs of a rapidly expanding operation.

Cash generation offers another measure of the expansion. Dell reported $4.288 billion in adjusted free cash flow for the quarter, a company-defined measure, alongside $4.459 billion in operating cash flow. It also said non-GAAP operating expenses fell to 7% of revenue. The figures indicate that scale and cost discipline are supporting profitability, but free cash flow should be assessed alongside working capital, inventory purchases and customer payment timing. TTN’s free cash flow guide explains why the measure requires scrutiny of its definition and reconciliation.

Dell’s Client Solutions Group provides a contrast. The business, which includes PCs, reported revenue of $12.9 billion, up 3%, while operating income fell 6% to $704 million. Enterprise infrastructure is driving the acceleration, while the traditional PC operation is growing more slowly. Dell’s board also approved an additional $25 billion share-repurchase authorization, and the company said it returned $4.9 billion to shareholders during the quarter. Buybacks do not remove the need to fund inventory, supplier commitments and customer support as shipments rise.

The investment case now rests on more than the headline backlog. Investors will need to track how much of the $132.3 billion converts into delivered systems, whether the infrastructure margin holds as volumes rise and whether operating cash flow keeps pace with reported earnings. Customers must show that AI workloads create enough value to justify continued spending on computing capacity. Dell’s results establish that the infrastructure cycle is producing substantial orders and revenue today. They do not yet prove how durable that spending will be or how much profit the wider AI supply chain can retain over time.

Profitability is a central part of the story. Dell’s Infrastructure Solutions Group generated $7.12 billion in operating income on $41.97 billion of revenue. The company said operating expenses, measured on a non-GAAP basis, had fallen to 7% of revenue. It also reported $4.29 billion in adjusted free cash flow for the quarter. Those figures indicate that the AI server expansion is not simply adding sales volume; it is currently contributing a larger share of operating profit.

The contrast with Dell’s PC business is substantial. Client Solutions Group revenue rose only 3% to $12.89 billion, while segment operating income fell 6% to $704 million. Consumer revenue declined 15%. The figures underline the company’s changing profile: enterprise infrastructure is driving growth while the traditional personal-computer business is expanding more slowly and facing different demand conditions.

Dell’s board also authorized an additional $25 billion in share repurchases. The company returned $4.9 billion to shareholders during the quarter through dividends and buybacks. The expanded authorization signals confidence in cash generation, but it does not remove the need to fund working capital, inventory and supply-chain commitments as server shipments rise.

The wider market remains less certain about the durability of AI spending. Investors have questioned whether cloud providers and large enterprises can sustain the current pace of capital expenditure, and recent volatility in semiconductor shares shows that strong results do not automatically translate into stable valuations. Dell shares had fallen sharply during the regular session before its earnings release, then rose in after-hours trading as investors assessed the new forecast, Reuters reported.

For suppliers, Dell’s results are evidence that AI infrastructure demand is translating into substantial orders and recognized revenue. For customers, the company’s backlog illustrates the scale of investment required to build and operate AI systems. For investors, the key questions are conversion, margins and cash collection: how quickly orders become shipped systems, whether component and power constraints raise costs, and whether customers generate enough value from AI workloads to justify continued spending.

Dell’s quarter strengthens the case that AI infrastructure is a major growth market. It does not settle the question of how profitable or durable that market will be. The next test is whether the record backlog converts into revenue and cash without eroding margins as the company scales.
