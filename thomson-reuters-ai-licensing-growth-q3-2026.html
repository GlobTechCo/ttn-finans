Thomson Reuters Raises Outlook as AI Licensing Grows

Thomson Reuters increased its 2026 revenue outlook as demand for professional software and AI licensing lifted quarterly results.

TTN Research Desk2026-11-03

Thomson Reuters has given investors a fresh example of how artificial intelligence can generate revenue for a company whose core asset is specialized information rather than raw computing power. The legal, tax and information-services group reported third-quarter revenue of $1.83 billion, a 10% increase, and raised its full-year organic growth outlook on November 3. Its results also exposed a less comfortable side of the transformation: bringing AI products to market can reduce margins even when customers are buying them.

Guidance improves after a stronger quarter

The company now expects 2026 organic revenue growth of 8.5% to 9%, up from an earlier expectation of approximately 8%. Organic growth attempts to isolate performance in the existing business by adjusting for currency and portfolio changes. It is therefore a more useful indicator of underlying customer demand than a simple comparison of reported revenue across periods that include acquisitions and disposals.

Thomson Reuters reported adjusted earnings of 81 cents per share. Its major professional-business groupings—legal, tax and accounting, and corporate services—recorded double-digit revenue growth. Much of the business relies on recurring subscriptions, which can give the company a more predictable revenue base than one-off software sales. The latest performance suggests that customers remain willing to spend on tools they consider essential to research, compliance and business workflows.

The financial presentation excludes the global print division after the company sold a majority stake. Thomson Reuters announced the completion of the sale of 51% of that business to investors advised by KKR, with gross proceeds of about $500 million. The change matters for interpreting guidance: investors should not assume that every headline growth number is calculated against an unchanged corporate portfolio.

Generative AI becomes a licensing business

One of the most striking figures concerns Reuters itself. News transaction revenue increased 56% to $44 million, supported by licensing activity connected with generative AI. The figure is not the total revenue of the Reuters news division, nor does it mean that all Reuters content is sold to AI developers. It shows that transactions involving news content are becoming a material growth channel at a time when publishers and software firms are negotiating how valuable information can be used in AI services.

Information licensing is commercially different from operating a consumer chatbot. Thomson Reuters controls a portfolio of content, structured data and professional workflow tools. That lets it seek value at several levels: licenses for information, recurring subscriptions for research platforms and software features that help users turn retrieved material into practical work. The key question is whether such products can command prices that compensate for the cost of developing and operating them.

The company highlighted enhancements to CoCounsel Legal and CoCounsel Tax, Audit and Accounting as part of its growth story. These products are intended to support professionals whose work depends on accurate, current and properly sourced information. In legal and tax environments, the cost of an incorrect answer can outweigh the convenience of rapid generation. That makes trustworthy data, permissions and human review central to the product design, not incidental marketing features.

TTN Finance has examined related enterprise adoption questions in its analysis of AI agents and business returns and the risks of granting AI systems operational access. Thomson Reuters occupies a particularly interesting position because its customers want both the automation benefits and the accountability of professional work.

Growth comes with a cost

There is no reason to assume the gains are effortless. Reuters reported that the operating margin in Thomson Reuters' legal division fell to 46.6%, from 49% in the comparable period, as AI-related investment weighed on costs. This is an important counterweight to the headline revenue gains. The company is spending to improve the platform while trying to maintain the economics of a subscription business.

The trade-off is familiar across technology, but the professional-information market has its own pressures. Competitors can use large language models to build interfaces quickly, while established providers must protect intellectual property, preserve reference quality and integrate new tools into existing customer routines. Developing a better answer generator alone may not be enough. The commercial value depends on whether a tool saves billable time, improves accuracy or lowers a client's compliance exposure.

There is also a competitive issue around licensing. AI developers need high-quality source material to improve retrieval and answer systems, and established information companies may seek compensation for that access. But the long-term size of the licensing market remains uncertain. Contracts can be renegotiated, model developers can change their content strategies, and regulation may affect what can be used and under what terms. One quarter of rapid transaction growth is evidence of momentum, not a guarantee of the industry's future economics.

What investors should measure

A more complete assessment requires three measures to be examined together. The first is recurring customer revenue and renewal rates, which show whether customers are keeping the software. The second is the incremental income generated by AI features and content licensing. The third is the cost required to build, operate and defend those offerings. Growth in one measure without clarity on the others can overstate the eventual benefit to shareholders.

Thomson Reuters also faces questions about investor expectations. Its shares had fallen 6.3% this year through November 2, compared with a roughly 10% gain in the S&P 500, according to Reuters. Relative share performance is not a direct verdict on product quality, but it indicates that a stronger financial report does not automatically settle valuation questions.

The significant development is that AI is beginning to appear in the financial statements of professional-information companies in identifiable ways: higher subscription growth, additional licensing revenue and measurable product-development costs. Thomson Reuters has improved its full-year outlook. The next test is whether the benefits become increasingly visible in margins as well as revenue—and whether customers continue paying for automation built on information they can trust.
