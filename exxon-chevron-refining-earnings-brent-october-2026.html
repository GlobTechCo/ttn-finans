Why Lower Crude Prices Can Coexist With Higher Refining Profits

Exxon and Chevron’s results show how scarce fuel-making capacity can lift refinery earnings even when the price of crude oil falls.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-31

ExxonMobil and Chevron reported stronger-than-expected quarterly earnings on October 30 as refining profits helped offset pressure from lower crude prices. Refiners can earn more when the difference between fuel prices and feedstock costs widens, even as the underlying oil benchmark declines. The results therefore tell a more complicated story than Brent prices alone.

Refining margins drove the story

A refinery earns money by processing crude oil into products that can be sold to wholesalers and distributors. Analysts often track the relationship between fuel prices and crude input costs through refining margins or crack spreads. These margins are not identical to a company’s realized profit because energy, labor, maintenance and logistics expenses still matter. Nevertheless, they provide a useful view of operating conditions. Supply disruptions affecting fuel production and transportation supported margins during the quarter.

ExxonMobil reported approximately $17.5 billion in adjusted earnings, or $4.23 per share, above analyst forecasts. It reported roughly $15.4 billion under generally accepted accounting principles. Those two measures should not be confused: adjustments can exclude items management considers unrepresentative of ongoing operations. The company also reported strong operating cash flow and substantial shareholder distributions, reflecting its ability to generate cash across different parts of the energy value chain.

Chevron reported approximately $10.4 billion in adjusted earnings, or $5.28 per share, beating the market consensus. Its reported earnings under GAAP were approximately $9.9 billion. The company highlighted strong utilization of its US refining system, which allowed it to capture favorable product margins. Utilization is particularly relevant when a disruption elsewhere reduces the amount of fuel available in the marketplace.

Why Brent and diesel can move differently

Brent is a benchmark for crude oil, not a direct price for the fuel a truck operator or airline purchases. Even if crude becomes cheaper, outages at refineries or restrictions on fuel supply can keep refined-product prices elevated. In that environment, integrated energy companies may experience weaker upstream margins but stronger downstream earnings. That diversification helped the two US majors during the period.

Consumers often expect gasoline prices to move immediately when oil prices decline. In practice, pump prices reflect refinery output, transport costs, inventories, taxes and retail conditions, as well as the price of the underlying crude. Diesel is especially important for freight, farming and industrial activity. Sustained shortages or high diesel prices can raise operating costs throughout the broader economy, even if the Brent benchmark does not rise by the same amount.

What investors should monitor

The durability of current margins is the central question. Repair work at damaged facilities, a shift in consumption or a change in trade flows could restore fuel supply and narrow the spread between refined products and crude. Meanwhile, an increase in Brent prices without a comparable increase in fuel prices could squeeze refiners even while helping upstream producers. Operational reliability, refinery maintenance schedules and cash flow after capital spending will also shape coming quarters.
