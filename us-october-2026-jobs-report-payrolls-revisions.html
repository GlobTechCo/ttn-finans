U.S. Adds 165,000 Jobs as Upward Revisions Strengthen the Hiring Picture

October hiring exceeded expectations, but a rise in involuntary part-time work complicates the message for households and the Federal Reserve.

TTN Research Desk2026-11-06

U.S. employers added 165,000 jobs in October, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on November 6, delivering a stronger hiring picture than economists had expected. Upward revisions to the preceding two months reinforced the improvement. The report gives businesses and policymakers a firmer employment backdrop, while leaving a less comfortable question about the quality and purchasing power of the work available.

The unemployment rate was 4.1%, down from September’s 4.2%. The Guardian’s live coverage reported a forecast of 65,000 additional jobs. That difference is a surprise relative to a forecast, rather than a measure of how far employment has exceeded the economy’s sustainable capacity. The two comparisons answer different questions and can lead to very different interpretations of the same headline.

Revisions make the improvement harder to dismiss

August’s payroll increase was revised to 186,000 from 133,000, and September’s to 60,000 from 29,000. Together, the revisions add 84,000 jobs to the previously reported total. BLS said professional and business services, health care, and transportation and warehousing added employment in October. Average hourly earnings increased 0.4% over the month and 3.1% over the year.

Revisions matter because the first payroll estimate is an early reading of an evolving dataset. Additional employer responses and updated seasonal factors can change the picture after financial markets have already reacted. Here, the direction of the revisions supports the stronger October number. It does not establish that every future revision will move the same way, or that one stronger month has become a durable hiring trend.

For interpreting the release, TTN’s guide to the payroll report and its two surveys explains a crucial distinction: jobs counted at establishments and people counted in households are different statistical units. Someone holding more than one payroll job can contribute differently to the two series. A change in payrolls consequently need not match the change in the number of employed people.

A low unemployment rate leaves some strain out of view

There was a weaker detail beneath the hiring gains. The number working part time for economic reasons rose by 339,000 to 4.8 million. These are people who wanted full-time employment but had reduced hours or could not find full-time jobs. The labor force participation rate was 61.9%, with BLS describing little monthly change.

That combination illustrates why the unemployment rate cannot carry the entire assessment of household conditions. A person with some paid work can be classified as employed while still lacking the hours needed to meet ordinary expenses. An employer adding a position and an existing worker losing hours can both appear in an economy whose aggregate payroll count is rising. These outcomes concern different dimensions of labor demand.

The household survey also distinguishes people actively seeking work from those outside the labor force. A lower unemployment rate can therefore have different meanings depending on participation and employment. TTN’s explanation of unemployment, underemployment and participation provides the relevant framework. In this release, the part-time measure is a reason to qualify the strong headline rather than disregard it.

For the Fed, stronger hiring changes the balance of risks

The Federal Reserve’s mandate includes maximum employment and stable prices. A firmer labor market can reduce the immediate concern that restrictive policy is producing a sharp employment contraction. It can also leave policymakers more room to confront inflation. Neither implication automatically dictates a rate increase or a rate cut; the decision depends on the broader evidence and the outlook when officials meet.

Wages belong in that assessment, but average hourly earnings are not a direct measure of every worker’s raise. Changes in the mix of industries and employees can influence the average. Nor does a nominal earnings increase establish an equivalent gain in purchasing power. A comparison with consumer prices needs matching periods and an appropriate inflation measure, as TTN’s inflation guide describes.

For companies, a stronger employment base can support demand while keeping staffing costs relevant to margins. Those effects differ by business model. A firm selling discretionary services may benefit from household income; a labor-intensive operator also faces the expense of delivering those services. The report alone cannot determine which effect dominates an individual company’s next earnings release.

The same tension matters for markets. Better growth can support expected corporate revenue, while a higher expected path for interest rates can reduce the present value investors assign to future cash flows. It is entirely possible for encouraging economic data to produce an unsettled trading response. October’s release improves the evidence on hiring; the accompanying hours, wages and future revisions will determine how much confidence that improvement deserves.
