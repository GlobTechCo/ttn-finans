Visa’s Quarterly Profit Reaches $6 Billion as Card Spending Keeps Growing

Revenue rose 13% in Visa’s fiscal fourth quarter. Payment volume and cross-border activity grew, while client incentives increased faster than revenue.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-27

Visa reported $6 billion of quarterly net income on Tuesday as spending through its card network continued to expand. Net revenue reached $12.1 billion, up 13% from a year earlier, in the fiscal fourth quarter ended September 30. The results show a payments business still growing even as investors look for signs of strain in consumer demand.

Reuters reported net income of $3.19 a share, compared with $2.62 a year earlier. Visa's issuer release puts adjusted, non-GAAP earnings at $3.48 a share. The two figures should remain separate: the adjusted measure excludes items that affect the financial statements, rather than representing an additional pool of profit earned on top of reported income.

The more useful question is what drove the expansion. Visa reports spending, transactions, international activity and the commercial incentives paid to clients. Those measures describe different parts of its business. Our guide to interpreting earnings explains why a strong headline profit does not remove the need to examine the operating figures underneath it.

Spending grew, but the pace eased

Payments volume increased 9% in constant dollars during July through September. That compares with 10% in the preceding quarter, a slowdown highlighted in Stock Titan's analysis of the release. These are year-over-year growth rates, not a statement that spending fell from one quarter to the next. Positive growth can become slower while the total level of activity continues to increase.

The constant-dollar comparison removes currency movements from the reported growth rate. It does not remove the effect of higher prices on the amount spent. Nor does it show whether every household is financially comfortable. Consumers can spend more in dollars because purchases cost more, because they buy more, or because their mix of purchases changes.

Our consumer-spending coverage makes a related distinction between broad totals and the experience of particular customers. A payment network sees transactions across many sellers. A retailer sees its own categories and customer base. Reading both can be useful, but neither should be presented as a complete household balance sheet or as a direct measure of the whole economy.

International activity has its own economics

Visa reported 14% growth in cross-border volume excluding transactions within Europe and 10% growth in processed transactions, both for the September quarter. The first measure concerns spending across borders; the second counts the transactions Visa processes. More transactions and larger payment amounts are different sources of activity, so the percentages need not match.

Timing also affects the revenue comparison. Visa recognizes service revenue using payment volume from the preceding quarter, while its other revenue categories reflect current-quarter activity. That means one earnings report combines information from different operating periods. A change in July-to-September spending does not flow through every revenue line at the same moment.

This helps explain why applying a single growth rate to the entire company can be misleading. The commercial mix, the timing of recognition and the terms of client relationships all affect how activity becomes revenue. Investors following the business need to track that conversion rather than assume that each extra dollar spent on a card produces an identical increase in Visa's earnings.

Client incentives show the cost of distribution

Client incentives increased 21% to $5.1 billion, faster than the quarter's 13% net revenue growth. The comparison deserves attention because incentives are part of the economics of attracting and retaining business. A larger network can still face competitive costs in securing the institutions and partners that bring transactions to it.

Visa's results differ from those of a company whose main activity is lending directly to cardholders. Our report on American Express illustrates why business models matter when comparing payment companies. Similar exposure to consumer activity does not make every revenue source, funding obligation or credit risk interchangeable.

For Visa, the next reports will show whether spending growth stabilizes and how much expanding activity remains after client incentives and operating costs. Tuesday's release supplies evidence of continued demand and rising profit. It also provides a more specific test for the coming quarters: the relationship between transaction growth, international activity and the revenue the network ultimately retains.
