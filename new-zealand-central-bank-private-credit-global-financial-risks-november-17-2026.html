New Zealand Central Bank Warns of Rising Private Credit Risks as Global Financial Fragility Grows

The Reserve Bank of New Zealand warns that expanding non-bank private credit, geopolitical tensions, and elevated AI valuations could amplify future economic shocks despite resilient domestic banks.

TTN Research DeskNov 17, 2026 · 2:19 PM EET

The Reserve Bank of New Zealand has warned that rising global financial risks and the growing role of non-bank lenders are creating vulnerabilities that could amplify a future economic shock. In its November Financial Stability Report, the RBNZ said the country's financial system remains resilient but highlighted private credit, geopolitical tensions and elevated technology valuations as areas requiring closer attention. Private credit expands beyond traditional banking

Private credit refers to loans provided by investment funds, asset managers and other non-bank institutions rather than traditional deposit-taking banks. Globally and across the Asia-Pacific region, the sector has expanded steadily over the past decade as tighter post-crisis bank capital rules encouraged commercial banks to pull back from higher-risk corporate and property lending, leaving room for private debt funds to step in.

Reuters reported from official data that direct finance and non-bank lending firms account for about 6% of overall lending in New Zealand. The RBNZ report separately said development lending by non-bank deposit takers has remained broadly unchanged at around 6% of the level of equivalent bank lending. These are different measures and should not be conflated. Non-bank lending remains small relative to bank lending, but the growing role of these institutions makes their activities increasingly important for financial supervision. Unlike regulated deposit-taking banks, non-bank lenders often face less standardized public reporting requirements, meaning information about underlying loan quality, borrower leverage and funds' ability to meet investor withdrawals can be less transparent.

Even a relatively small lending segment can transmit broader financial stress if its borrowers, institutional investors or wholesale funding providers are closely connected to domestic and international banks. When banks provide credit lines or leverage to private debt funds, losses in non-bank portfolios can indirectly affect the regulated banking system.

Australian property stress offers a warning

The report pointed to difficulties in Australia's property development market as an example of how private credit vulnerabilities can emerge during a downturn. The failure of a major developer exposed private lenders to potential losses, while some investment funds restricted withdrawals as redemption requests increased.

Such redemption gates illustrate a core structural challenge in private credit markets: liquidity mismatch. Private credit loans are typically illiquid, bespoke instruments that are held to maturity and difficult to sell quickly at fair value during periods of stress, whereas some open-ended or semi-liquid fund structures offer investors periodic redemption windows. Commercial property and construction finance are particularly sensitive to elevated interest rates, rising building costs and softer presales or leasing demand, which can delay project completion and strain borrower cash flows.

The RBNZ did not describe New Zealand's private credit sector as a systemic threat. However, it argued that the expansion of non-bank finance warrants continued supervisory monitoring, particularly as offshore private credit managers and international lenders become more active in domestic financing deals.

AI investment creates another channel of exposure

The central bank also highlighted financial stability risks connected with rapid global investment in artificial intelligence infrastructure. AI-related equity valuations remain elevated despite recent market pullbacks, leaving asset prices vulnerable if earnings growth disappoints. A slowdown in capital expenditure or weaker-than-expected commercial profitability could generate losses for lenders, private credit funds and institutional investors with concentrated exposure.

Building AI infrastructure requires substantial upfront capital for hyperscale data centers, specialized computing equipment, electricity generation and high-capacity networking. The RBNZ warned that the growing use of debt to finance AI-related investment can increase leverage and exposures for lenders and investors. It also highlighted financing arrangements in which AI firms provide funding to their customers, creating links across the AI value chain. If investment slows or expected profitability fails to materialize, lenders, private credit funds and other investors with concentrated exposures could face losses.

Cross-border financial links between AI-related businesses could amplify a market correction, transmitting stress across technology firms, infrastructure developers, energy suppliers, commercial banks and private credit funds.

New Zealand banks remain well capitalized

Despite these external and structural concerns, the RBNZ emphasized that New Zealand's core banking system remains in a strong position. Domestic banks maintain solid capital and liquidity buffers above regulatory minimums, while central bank stress tests indicate that banks and insurers could withstand severe economic and financial shocks, including sharp rises in unemployment or property price declines. At the same time, smaller domestic businesses and sectors dependent on discretionary household spending continue to face pressure from tight financial conditions. Broader global instability or trade fragmentation could affect New Zealand through weaker demand from major trading partners, reduced agricultural and export income, and higher offshore wholesale funding costs for local lenders.

Regulatory focus shifts toward less visible risks

The report underscores a growing challenge for financial authorities: traditional banking ratios and prudential indicators may not capture vulnerabilities building outside the regulated perimeter. Private credit and AI infrastructure financing can support economic activity and diversify funding sources, but rapid expansion can create concentrated exposures, opaque leverage and liquidity mismatches that become harder to manage when credit conditions tighten.

For institutional and retail investors, the RBNZ's assessment reinforces the importance of examining underlying loan structures, fund liquidity terms and counterparty linkages alongside headline bank stability and technology sector performance. The resilience of individual commercial banks does not eliminate credit or valuation risks elsewhere in the global financial system.
