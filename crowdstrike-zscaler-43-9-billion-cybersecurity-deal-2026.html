CrowdStrike’s $43.9 Billion Zscaler Deal Would Reshape the Cybersecurity Market

CrowdStrike’s proposed $43.9 billion Zscaler takeover would combine endpoint protection with cloud-based zero-trust security for enterprise customers.

TTN Research DeskNov 24, 2026 · 8:13 AM EST

CrowdStrike has agreed to acquire Zscaler in an all-stock transaction valued at approximately $43.9 billion, bringing two major cybersecurity businesses under one corporate structure. The proposed deal would combine CrowdStrike’s endpoint and workload protection with Zscaler’s cloud-based network security and zero-trust access technology. If completed, it would create one of the sector’s largest cybersecurity platforms at a time when companies are trying to secure increasingly distributed systems and the growing use of artificial-intelligence agents. Under the agreement announced on November 23, Zscaler shareholders will receive 1.135 shares of CrowdStrike common stock for each Zscaler share. Based on CrowdStrike’s closing price on November 20, the exchange ratio implies a value of approximately $260.41 per Zscaler share, representing a premium of about 34% to Zscaler’s closing price on that date. Existing CrowdStrike shareholders are expected to own approximately 84.1% of the combined company after completion, with Zscaler shareholders holding the remaining 15.9%.

The transaction has been approved by both companies’ boards, but it is not yet complete. It remains subject to Zscaler shareholder approval, regulatory clearances and customary closing conditions. CrowdStrike expects the deal to close in the second half of its fiscal 2028. Until then, the companies will continue to operate independently. Zscaler founder and chief executive Jay Chaudhry is expected to continue leading the Zscaler business after closing and join CrowdStrike’s board.

The strategic logic is based on the changing architecture of corporate technology. CrowdStrike built its Falcon platform around protecting endpoints, servers, cloud workloads and other enterprise systems. Zscaler focuses on securing connections between users, applications and cloud services, using a zero-trust model that verifies access rather than relying on a traditional trusted corporate network.

Combining those capabilities could give customers a broader security environment in which activity across devices, identities, workloads and network connections can be analysed together. That matters as companies move applications and data across multiple cloud providers, support remote workforces and deploy AI systems that can access enterprise information and execute tasks. AI agents create additional security questions because they may interact with applications and data at machine speed, sometimes through credentials and permissions that are difficult for traditional tools to monitor.

CrowdStrike is presenting the acquisition as a way to build what it calls Zero Trust for AI Safety. The company says Zscaler would add access to approximately 8,000 customers and expand its total addressable market by more than $185 billion. It expects the combined business to begin with more than $10 billion in annual recurring revenue. These are company projections and strategic estimates, not guaranteed results from the transaction.

The financial position of CrowdStrike helps explain why it is pursuing such a large acquisition. In its third-quarter fiscal 2027 results, also released on November 23, the company reported revenue of $1.58 billion for the three months ended October 31, up 28% from a year earlier. Subscription revenue increased 29% to $1.50 billion. Annual recurring revenue reached $6.26 billion, an increase of 27%, while net new annual recurring revenue added during the quarter was $419.2 million, up 58% year over year.

CrowdStrike also reported operating cash flow of $665 million and free cash flow of $474 million for the quarter. Its GAAP subscription gross margin was 79%, compared with 78% a year earlier. The results show a business with substantial recurring revenue and strong cash generation, although the proposed purchase would be far larger than its quarterly operating scale.

The acquisition could strengthen CrowdStrike’s competitive position against established cybersecurity providers and newer companies offering specialised AI security products. A more integrated platform may help the company sell additional modules to existing customers, reduce the number of separate security vendors that large organisations need to manage and improve the value of data collected across different parts of an IT environment.

But integration will be difficult. CrowdStrike and Zscaler have different products, customer relationships and technology architectures. Combining them without disrupting service, slowing product development or confusing customers will require sustained investment. Enterprise cybersecurity buyers also tend to evaluate vendors carefully because a change in security infrastructure can affect access, compliance and business continuity.

The all-stock structure creates another consideration for investors. CrowdStrike is not funding the purchase with a fixed cash payment, but it is committing to issue a significant number of shares. The final economic value of the transaction will therefore move with CrowdStrike’s share price until completion. Existing Zscaler investors will become shareholders in the combined company, while current CrowdStrike shareholders will own a smaller percentage of a larger business.

Regulatory scrutiny is also possible given the scale of the transaction and the importance of cybersecurity services to major enterprises. The companies will need to demonstrate that the deal can deliver its promised benefits without reducing competition or creating problems for customers that depend on their products.

For CrowdStrike, the acquisition is a bet that customers will increasingly prefer an integrated security architecture and that the company can turn that preference into higher recurring revenue. For Zscaler shareholders, the deal offers a premium and participation in the combined business. For the wider market, the proposed $43.9 billion transaction is a sign that cybersecurity consolidation is accelerating as AI changes both the scale and the nature of enterprise security risks.
