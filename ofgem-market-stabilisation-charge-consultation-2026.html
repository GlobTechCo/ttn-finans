UK Energy Regulator Proposes Payments Between Suppliers When Customers Switch

Ofgem wants to activate a dormant safeguard against sharp wholesale-price falls. The proposal raises a trade-off between supplier resilience and competition for cheaper deals.

TTN Research DeskOct 16, 2026 · 1:08 PM EDT

Britain’s energy regulator Ofgem proposed Friday to activate a mechanism requiring a supplier that gains a customer to compensate the supplier losing that account when wholesale prices fall sharply. The consultation on the Market Stabilisation Charge runs until October 29. Activation remains a proposal, rather than a completed decision.

Ofgem says the safeguard is intended to protect customers by reducing instability among suppliers that buy energy in advance. The licence condition was reintroduced earlier this year in dormant form. The regulator is now asking whether market conditions justify turning it on.

The problem created by falling prices

A supplier can purchase energy ahead of delivery to reduce uncertainty over its costs. If wholesale prices subsequently fall, a competitor may be able to offer a cheaper deal. Customers who switch leave the original supplier holding energy purchased at a higher price for accounts it no longer serves.

The proposed mechanism addresses that mismatch through a payment between suppliers. Ofgem says payments would arise only if specified wholesale-price conditions were met. Its statement also says activation would not automatically raise customer bills or immediately create a charge.

Stability has a competitive cost to consider

The economic trade-off is straightforward. Protecting a supplier against part of its hedging losses may reduce the risk that prudent advance purchases become financially damaging. At the same time, requiring the winning supplier to pay for a new account can change how much it is willing to spend attracting that customer.

That does not establish how any particular tariff would change. It does mean the design needs to be judged on more than the number of supplier failures it might prevent. The strength of competition, the availability of cheaper offers and the size of the transfer all matter to households.

For an American reader, the distinction is useful: this is a proposed intervention in Britain’s retail energy market. It is not a new US utility rule, a direct household switching tax or a general guarantee against higher energy prices.

The design must specify who pays and under what conditions

A payment between suppliers is different from a charge collected directly from a switching household. The economic burden can still influence offers if it changes the gaining supplier's expected cost of acquiring an account. Whether and how that effect reaches a particular customer depends on the final design and competition. It cannot be read directly from the mechanism's name.

The trigger matters because wholesale prices can decline without leaving every supplier with the same exposure. Suppliers may have purchased different amounts at different times and may serve different customer groups. A rule based on a common market measure needs to balance administrability against that variation. The consultation is an opportunity to assess those design choices rather than evidence that every affected supplier would otherwise fail.

Deactivation is important for the same reason. A temporary safeguard that remains active after exceptional conditions pass could continue to affect competition. Clear entry and exit conditions allow suppliers and customers to understand when the transfers can apply. The proposed dates, thresholds and notice arrangements should therefore be evaluated together.

For households comparing offers, the relevant facts remain the actual tariff terms, applicable contract conditions and the regulator's eventual decision. This consultation does not establish that switching has become uneconomic for everyone. The immediate policy development is the proposal to activate a dormant mechanism, with its practical consequences still dependent on the consultation and implementation.

What the consultation must resolve

Ofgem is seeking feedback on activation and operation, including triggers, notice periods and deactivation arrangements. Those details determine when an exceptional safeguard starts to influence everyday pricing. A mechanism that activates too readily would have a different competitive effect from one reserved for unusually sharp market moves.

TTN’s explanation of how cost shocks spread provides the wider context. Supplier stability can limit disruption, while competition can help transmit lower costs to customers. The consultation concerns how to balance those objectives; it does not yet demonstrate the outcome.
