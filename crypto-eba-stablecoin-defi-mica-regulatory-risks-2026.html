EU Banking Watchdog Flags Stablecoin Risks as DeFi Tests the Limits of MiCA Rules

EU regulators warn that stablecoin use in DeFi is testing consumer safeguards and the boundaries of MiCA.

The European Banking Authority has warned that stablecoins are becoming more deeply embedded in decentralised finance, creating consumer-protection and regulatory challenges for the European Union. In a report published on November 25, the watchdog said dollar- and euro-linked tokens are used as settlement assets, trading liquidity and collateral across decentralised exchanges and lending protocols.

The report does not describe DeFi as a large part of the overall crypto market. The value of assets locked in DeFi represented about 3% of total crypto-asset market capitalisation in the EBA’s analysis. The EBA also said estimated EU user engagement remains limited, partly because self-hosted wallets make activity difficult to attribute. But the authority said access is becoming easier as crypto-asset service providers offer interfaces to decentralised protocols and AI-enabled tools reduce the technical steps required to use them. That shift matters for the EU’s Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, known as MiCA. The framework regulates the issuance and distribution of asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens, as well as crypto-asset services. Fully decentralised services without an intermediary sit outside parts of that regulatory perimeter, creating questions about how rules apply when regulated companies help customers reach decentralised platforms.

Stablecoins are the plumbing of DeFi

Stablecoins are designed to maintain a relatively stable value against a reference asset, most commonly the US dollar or euro. In DeFi, they serve several functions: traders use them to move between crypto assets, liquidity providers deposit them into trading pools, and lending protocols use them as assets that can be borrowed or supplied as collateral.

The EBA’s review found that USDC was the most widely used MiCA-authorised e-money token in the DeFi protocols it examined. As of July 2026, USDC had a market capitalisation of $73 billion. Its liquidity helped make it an important settlement asset on decentralised exchanges and the most borrowed MiCA e-money token across the lending protocols in the sample.

These figures are snapshots from the report’s July 2026 data, not current market-cap estimates. Their significance is structural: a relatively small number of stablecoins provide much of the liquidity that supports a diverse set of protocols.

The regulatory gap is not only about issuers

MiCA sets requirements for authorised stablecoin issuers and crypto-asset service providers. It also prohibits issuers and service providers from paying interest on asset-referenced tokens and e-money tokens. DeFi protocols, however, can allow users to deposit stablecoins into lending pools and receive variable returns generated by borrowing demand and protocol rules.

The EBA said some crypto-asset service providers appear to facilitate access to DeFi or market structured exposure to it. This creates a potential regulatory-arbitrage problem: a customer may obtain access to yield-generating stablecoin strategies through a decentralised protocol even though similar interest payments are restricted for regulated providers under MiCA.

The EBA reiterated that the European Commission should assess whether additional requirements are needed for crypto-asset service providers that facilitate customer access to DeFi lending protocols as part of the review of MiCA. Possible measures discussed in the broader regulatory debate include stronger risk disclosures, suitability checks and limits on access to high-risk products.

DeFi can transmit stress back to stablecoin issuers

The EBA’s concern extends beyond compliance. If a protocol holds a large amount of a reserve-backed stablecoin, losses or a sudden loss of confidence could prompt users to withdraw funds or seek redemptions.

That can create a channel from DeFi into centralised finance. Stablecoin issuers, custodians and exchanges may face reputational pressure or a surge in redemption requests even if the original incident occurred in a separate protocol. The EBA pointed to the April 2026 Drift protocol exploit on Solana, which caused losses of around $285 million. A large portion of the stolen assets was converted into USDC, and the incident prompted criticism of Circle’s response before Drift later migrated its primary settlement layer from USDC to USDT.

The example shows why stablecoin risk cannot be assessed solely by looking at the issuer’s reserve assets. The token may be backed by reserves, but the way it is used across protocols can create operational and liquidity connections that are harder to monitor.

What the report means for the crypto industry

The EBA’s findings do not amount to a new ban on DeFi or a final amendment to MiCA. They are a supervisory assessment and a signal that regulators are watching the links between decentralised protocols and regulated intermediaries more closely.

For stablecoin issuers, the challenge is to maintain confidence in reserves while monitoring how tokens are used outside their direct platforms. For exchanges and other crypto-asset service providers, facilitating DeFi access may bring customer demand but also create new compliance and conduct obligations. For users, a stablecoin’s peg does not remove smart-contract, liquidity, custody or regulatory risks associated with the protocol in which it is deposited. The policy question for Europe is how to preserve innovation without allowing regulated firms to outsource high-risk activity to structures that fall outside the same safeguards. As DeFi becomes easier to access, the boundary between centralised and decentralised finance may become less meaningful to customers — and more important to supervisors.
