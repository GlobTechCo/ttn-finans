Intel’s Server Boom Meets a Weaker Outlook and a Factory Disruption

Intel’s data-center sales surged, but its next-quarter forecast disappointed. A factory disruption adds another constraint as server demand remains strong.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-29

Intel reported a surge in data-center sales Thursday but forecast a weaker fourth quarter, exposing the gap between demand for server processors and its ability to convert that demand into sustained growth. A factory disruption and pressure in the personal-computer market complicate the chipmaker’s near-term outlook.

Third-quarter revenue rose 21% to $16.5 billion, while adjusted earnings reached 52 cents a share. Intel’s fourth-quarter forecast called for $15 billion to $16 billion in sales and adjusted earnings of 25 cents a share. Reuters reported analyst expectations of $17.07 billion and 43 cents, respectively, using LSEG estimates.

The revenue midpoint of $15.5 billion implies a decline of roughly 6% from the reported quarter, a calculation using rounded company figures. That is a sequential comparison, not a forecast of the year-over-year change. It explains why a strong quarter and a disappointing outlook can coexist in the same announcement.

Server demand is outrunning the PC business

Intel’s data-center and artificial-intelligence segment produced $7.3 billion in sales, up 78% from a year earlier. Its client-computing and physical-AI division, which includes PC-related products, generated $8.1 billion, down 5%. The divergence leaves two large parts of the company moving in opposite directions.

Server processors perform general computing and coordinate work across data-center systems. Demand associated with AI infrastructure can therefore benefit the processor business without making every server chip a specialized AI accelerator. The distinction helps readers understand why a broad computing supplier can participate in the expansion beyond the most prominently discussed training chips.

Intel’s presentation linked pressure in the PC market to industry memory shortages and said the downturn was expected to persist through at least the first half of 2027. That is management’s assessment, rather than an independently established forecast for every computer manufacturer. It nevertheless limits how quickly improving server sales can translate into balanced company growth.

The problem is not simply finding customers. Product availability determines how much demand becomes revenue within a reporting period. TTN’s coverage of AI-related chip supply constraints explains why more orders across the industry do not immediately mean more finished processors reaching buyers.

A factory interruption creates a separate constraint

Chief Financial Officer David Zinsner told Reuters that an explosion at Intel’s Kiryat Gat facility in Israel had stopped production for about two weeks. He estimated a $300 million fourth-quarter revenue impact and a similar effect in the following quarter. The disruption was also expected to reduce fourth-quarter gross margin by about two percentage points.

That interview supplies a specific explanation for part of the forecast weakness; it does not establish that the entire gap to analyst estimates comes from the incident. A production interruption, softer PC conditions and the product mix are separate pressures, and their effects should not be collapsed into a single cause.

An expected revenue impact is also different from the cost of repairing a facility. Lost or delayed shipments can reduce sales, while underused equipment and production expenses can affect margins. The company’s estimates should not be presented as a verified physical-damage bill or a final insurance claim.

Factory economics still matter after the sales surge

Intel’s earnings presentation showed a $1.9 billion operating loss in its foundry segment, improved from $2.3 billion a year earlier. The segment’s $6.7 billion in revenue includes internal business, with substantial intersegment eliminations in the consolidated accounts. It is not evidence that outside manufacturing customers bought $6.7 billion of services.

That accounting boundary is essential to judging the manufacturing strategy. A company can report higher internal factory revenue as its product divisions purchase more chips, without demonstrating the same increase in independent customer demand. Consolidated results eliminate transactions within the group so they are not counted twice.

For investors assessing returns on AI-related capital spending, Thursday’s results supply both progress and unresolved costs. Server growth supports the demand case, while foundry losses and production constraints show why additional revenue does not automatically produce a comparable improvement in cash generation.

The next earnings report will test shipment recovery, the balance between PC and server sales, and the margin consequences of the disruption. Those are measurable developments. Strong demand alone cannot settle how much Intel will deliver, when customers will receive it or how profitable that production will be.
