Eric Schmidt Reportedly Holds 1% of Anthropic as IPO Plans Draw Attention

A reported early investment by Google's former CEO highlights the value and uncertainty surrounding private AI companies ahead of possible public listings.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-20

Eric Schmidt, the former chief executive of Google, reportedly owns about 1% of Anthropic, the developer of Claude. Bloomberg's October 19 report, carried by Reuters, describes an early investment that could become extremely valuable if the AI company proceeds with plans to list its shares.

Schmidt reportedly committed tens of millions of dollars in an early financing round. Whether he has sold any part of the position since then is unclear. The reported holding is therefore not the same as a confirmed, currently liquid fortune.

How a 1% stake could be valued

At a hypothetical $1.5 trillion company valuation, a one-percent position would be worth $15 billion on paper. That arithmetic is useful for understanding the scale of private-market expectations, but it is not an independently verified estimate of what Schmidt could sell or receive.

The outcome would depend on Anthropic's eventual share count, offering price, trading performance, lockup arrangements and any transactions by existing investors. A contemplated valuation is not the price of a completed IPO.

Why Anthropic attracts investors

Anthropic sells access to Claude models through subscriptions, developer services and enterprise products. Scaling those services requires substantial spending on computing capacity, including partnerships with established cloud providers.

That combination creates the central tension in the investment case: rapidly growing demand for AI services can expand revenue, but the cost of training models and serving customer requests also remains material. TTN's earlier coverage of AI inference economics explains why usage growth does not automatically translate into attractive margins.

What an IPO could reveal

A public listing could give investors more standardized financial disclosures, including revenue composition, operating losses, cash flow and infrastructure commitments. Until those documents exist, comparisons between private AI developers and public technology companies remain incomplete.

For investors assessing any eventual share offering, company valuation should be considered alongside the durability of customer demand, pricing power and the pace of investment. Returns earned by early backers do not indicate what later public shareholders will earn.

The reported Schmidt investment is a window into the extraordinary expectations surrounding the AI sector, not proof of an imminent listing or an assured windfall.
