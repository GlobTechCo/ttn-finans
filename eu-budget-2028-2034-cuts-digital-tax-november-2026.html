EU Budget Proposal Cuts €40 Billion as Brussels Considers 3% Digital Tax on Big Tech

Costa's revised EU budget cuts €40 billion and proposes a 3% levy on major digital revenues.

TTN Research DeskNov 21, 2026 · 1:56 PM EST

European Union leaders are facing a difficult financial choice as negotiations over the bloc's next seven-year budget enter a decisive phase. A revised proposal from European Council President Antonio Costa would reduce planned spending by €40 billion while introducing a potential digital tax on large technology companies operating across the European market.

The proposal, reported by Reuters on November 21, would set the EU's 2028–2034 budget at €1.582 trillion. That is €40 billion below the compromise proposal presented by the Irish EU presidency in October and €180.6 billion below the European Commission's original plan.

The changes matter because the budget determines how much funding Europe can direct toward industrial competitiveness, defence, agriculture, research, infrastructure and regional development during a period of increasing economic and geopolitical pressure.

A smaller budget and difficult spending choices

Costa's proposal does not distribute the reductions evenly. The European Competitiveness Fund, designed to strengthen European industry against competition from the United States and China, would face significant reductions, while defence and external spending would also be affected.

Agriculture and cohesion funding, which supports economic development across EU regions, would receive relatively greater protection compared with the Irish presidency's earlier compromise. European governments have increasingly argued that the continent needs additional investment in advanced manufacturing, strategic technologies and defence capabilities, and reducing the resources available for those priorities could complicate efforts to strengthen Europe's industrial position.

The European Parliament has already expressed opposition to the revised figures. Budget negotiator Siegfried Muresan described the proposal as unacceptable and argued that reductions would undermine the investment purpose of the EU budget. France has also criticised the proposal, particularly over the level of protection offered to agricultural funding.

These disagreements demonstrate that negotiations are not simply about reducing expenditure. They involve deciding which industries, regions and political priorities should receive limited European resources across global financial markets.

A proposed 3% tax on major digital companies

The most commercially significant element of Costa's proposal could be a new EU digital tax. The plan would introduce a 3% levy on annual net turnover generated from three activities: online advertising, digital intermediation services and user-generated data.

It would apply to companies with annual digital activity turnover exceeding €50 million within the EU and global turnover above €750 million. The thresholds would place several major international technology groups within the potential scope of the proposal, including Google, Microsoft, Meta, Apple and Amazon.

Unlike conventional corporate income taxation, which is generally based on taxable profits, the proposed levy would target qualifying digital turnover. This distinction matters because companies can report substantial revenue from European users while recognising profits across different jurisdictions, meaning a turnover-based tax could create additional costs even for businesses operating with relatively narrow margins.

For advertising platforms, the financial consequences would depend on the amount of qualifying revenue generated in Europe and the ability to pass additional costs to advertisers. Digital marketplaces and intermediary platforms could face a different calculation, depending on which services fall within the final legal definition, though the proposal still requires political agreement among EU governments.

Why the United States could become involved

The proposed levy also creates a potential trade-policy problem. Many of the companies likely to fall within its scope are headquartered in the United States, and President Donald Trump has previously opposed European digital taxation measures alongside US tax and infrastructure policy disputes.

If adopted, the tax could become part of a broader disagreement over market access, technology regulation and the treatment of American companies operating in Europe. For the EU, additional revenue could reduce pressure on national contributions and provide another funding source for common policies, whereas technology companies face higher compliance costs and potential adjustments to advertising prices or service fees.

Debt repayments create another budget constraint

Costa's proposal also addresses the repayment of the EU's post-pandemic recovery borrowing. The bloc is expected to have jointly borrowed more than €600 billion by the end of 2026 for its recovery fund, with repayment of principal and interest scheduled to begin in 2028 and continue until 2058.

The revised proposal would slow the repayment of principal during the first seven years, leaving more resources available for other budget priorities. This would not eliminate the debt or cancel repayment obligations, but it would change the timing of payments and shift financial pressure across future budgets while combining spending reductions, potential taxation and repayment adjustments.

What happens next

EU leaders are expected to discuss Costa's proposal at a special summit on November 26–27 to narrow differences between net contributors and net recipients. An agreement is targeted for December, although the final budget and its revenue sources require unanimous approval from member states, giving individual governments substantial negotiating power.

Businesses in digital advertising, cloud services, online marketplaces, manufacturing and defence will be watching the final allocation of resources closely, alongside broader European trade and economic policy. Until governments and the European Parliament reach agreement, the €1.582 trillion figure and the proposed 3% digital levy remain negotiating positions rather than final policy.
