Wall Street Sell-Off Deepens as Dow Plunges 1,100 Points and AI Fears Spread

The Dow fell 1,101 points as Wall Street extended its sell-off and investors reassessed AI valuations.

TTN Research DeskNov 20, 2026 · 4:37 AM ET

Market Analysis · November 20, 2026 · 4:37 AM ET

Wall Street suffered another broad sell-off on Thursday, November 19, as investors continued reducing exposure to technology stocks and reassessed the financial returns expected from artificial intelligence investments. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell more than 1,100 points, while the S&P 500 recorded its sixth consecutive daily decline, extending a market retreat that has increasingly spread beyond the technology sector. The S&P 500 dropped 2% to 7,175.83, its longest losing streak since 2024. The Dow Jones Industrial Average declined 1,101.11 points, or 2.2%, to 49,097.06, while the Nasdaq Composite lost 2.3% to 23,879.20. The Russell 2000 also fell 2.2% to 2,655.11.

More than 90% of the companies in the S&P 500 finished lower. The benchmark index is now 8.2% below its record set in October, bringing the market closer to correction territory.

The decline continued despite a successful stock market debut by artificial intelligence company Anthropic. The contrast suggests that investors are becoming more selective about AI-related valuations rather than treating every development in the sector as a reason to buy technology shares.

A broad decline reaches beyond technology

The weekly performance has become increasingly concerning. Through Thursday's close, the Dow was down 6.2% for the week, while the S&P 500 had lost 5.1%. The Nasdaq was lower by 5%, and the Russell 2000 had declined 4.9%, according to Associated Press market data.

Despite these losses, all four indexes remained positive for 2026. The S&P 500 was still up 4.8% for the year, while the Nasdaq had gained 2.7%.

AI investment returns become a central concern

Technology companies have committed enormous amounts of capital to data centres, advanced processors, networking equipment and computing infrastructure. These investments are intended to support AI model development and the growing use of commercial AI services.

Building additional computing capacity requires substantial upfront spending. Companies must finance equipment, electricity, facilities and operating costs before all of that capacity can be converted into paying customer demand.

The market is no longer evaluating AI businesses solely on the basis of technological progress. Investors are increasingly looking for evidence that spending is translating into sustainable operating cash flow and attractive returns on invested capital.

Anthropic's debut fails to lift the broader market

Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 per share. The stock opened at $111.50 and finished its first trading session at $97.17, approximately 17.1% above the offering price.

However, the positive reception did not reverse the broader market decline. Technology shares continued to face selling pressure as investors considered whether the valuations of companies involved in artificial intelligence already reflected an unusually optimistic growth outlook.

Interest rates add another layer of uncertainty

Higher borrowing costs can reduce the present value of future corporate earnings, making expensive growth stocks particularly sensitive to changes in interest-rate expectations.

Companies investing heavily in infrastructure can also face higher financing costs, especially when projects require years of spending before generating their full economic returns.

Treasury market movements provide another indication of investor positioning. During periods of uncertainty, investors may move toward government securities, influencing bond prices and yields.

However, falling yields do not necessarily provide immediate support for equities. If yields decline because investors expect weaker economic activity, concerns about future corporate earnings can outweigh the potential benefit of lower financing costs.

The Federal Reserve's next policy decisions will therefore remain important for market participants. Investors will continue evaluating inflation, employment conditions and economic growth to determine how much room policymakers have to adjust interest rates.

What the sell-off means for the U.S. economy

A decline in stock prices does not necessarily indicate that the U.S. economy is entering a recession.

Equity markets reflect expectations about future profits, interest rates and investor risk tolerance. These expectations can change considerably before economic activity responds.

Nevertheless, a prolonged decline can have broader consequences.

Falling equity valuations may reduce household wealth, particularly among investors holding stocks through retirement accounts. Companies may also become more cautious about issuing shares, financing acquisitions or committing to large expansion projects.

Smaller businesses can be particularly sensitive to tighter financial conditions because they generally have fewer funding options than large corporations.

The decline in the Russell 2000 suggests that concerns are not limited to the largest technology companies. Investors are also reassessing the outlook for smaller U.S. businesses.

At the same time, the fact that major indexes remain positive for the year provides important context. The current downturn is significant, but it has not yet eliminated the gains recorded earlier in 2026.

What investors will watch next

The next sessions will help establish whether Thursday's decline represents an acceleration of an existing correction or a temporary period of unusually intense selling.

Investors will monitor technology earnings, corporate guidance, capital expenditure plans and evidence of commercial demand for AI services.

For major technology companies, the ability to demonstrate measurable returns from infrastructure spending may become increasingly important. Revenue growth alone may no longer be sufficient to satisfy investors if operating costs and capital requirements continue rising.

Market breadth will also deserve attention. A recovery led by a small number of large technology stocks would provide a different signal from a rebound involving a wider range of companies.

The latest sell-off does not prove that the AI investment cycle has ended. It does show that investors are becoming less willing to overlook valuation and financing risks.

For Wall Street, the central issue is shifting from how quickly artificial intelligence can expand to how much sustainable profit that expansion can ultimately generate.
