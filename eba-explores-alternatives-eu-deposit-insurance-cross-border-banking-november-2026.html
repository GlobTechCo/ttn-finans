European Banking Watchdog Explores Alternatives to EU-Wide Deposit Insurance

European regulators are examining narrower ways to support cross-border banks as disagreement continues to block a common EU deposit insurance system.

TTN Research DeskNov 15, 2026

The European Union’s long-stalled deposit insurance plan may give way to narrower measures intended to ease cross-border banking. The European Banking Authority and European Commission are examining options that could require parent banks to support foreign subsidiaries during a crisis, encourage lenders to use branches rather than separately capitalised subsidiaries, or create a two-tier guarantee structure, the Financial Times reported on November 15.

The discussions do not amount to an agreed policy or a formal replacement proposal. They reflect the difficulty of completing the EU banking union’s third pillar after years of disagreement over how deposit risks should be shared between member states. The immediate question is whether practical changes to bank structures and crisis support can improve the single market without first resolving the political dispute over a common insurance fund.

Why the original plan remains blocked

The European Commission proposed a European Deposit Insurance Scheme, known as EDIS, in November 2015. It was intended to complete the banking union alongside common supervision and bank-resolution arrangements. Under the proposal, national deposit guarantee schemes would gradually be supported by a European layer, with the ultimate aim of making protection less dependent on the country where a bank is headquartered.

The existing system already protects eligible deposits of up to €100,000 per depositor, per bank, through national guarantee schemes across the EU. EDIS would not simply raise that limit. Its purpose would be to make the funding behind the guarantee more resilient when a bank failure places heavy pressure on one national system.

The political obstacle has been the distribution of losses. Governments in countries with stronger banking systems have resisted arrangements that could expose their taxpayers or deposit guarantee funds to losses arising from banks in other countries. Other policymakers argue that without a shared backstop, the banking union remains incomplete and national banking systems can remain tied too closely to the creditworthiness of their sovereign governments.

Parent-bank support and branches offer narrower routes

One option under examination would make a parent bank’s commitment to support a foreign subsidiary legally enforceable. A cross-border banking group could be required to provide funds to a subsidiary if that entity came under stress, with supervisors able to enforce the commitment.

The approach would address one weakness of a fragmented banking market: a group can operate across several countries, but its legal entities and liquidity may still be separated by national rules. In a crisis, that separation can complicate the movement of funds precisely when a subsidiary needs support. A binding commitment could make the parent’s responsibility clearer, although it would not automatically resolve every question about which national authority controls the funds or how losses are allocated. Another option would encourage banks to use branches instead of separately incorporated subsidiaries in some markets. A branch is part of the same legal bank as its head office, while a subsidiary is a distinct legal entity with its own capital and regulatory obligations. Branch structures can therefore make some cross-border operations simpler, but they also concentrate responsibility in the home country and require confidence that the parent bank and its supervisor can manage risks across the group.

Neither approach is equivalent to common deposit insurance. They could make it easier for banks to operate across borders and support foreign operations, but they would not create a single European fund that automatically pays depositors when a bank fails.

A possible two-tier guarantee system

The Commission is also considering a structure under which larger banks could receive coverage at European level while smaller institutions remain under national guarantee schemes. A liquidity backstop could connect the two layers, allowing support to move when a national system faces pressure.

The central unresolved issue is whether the European layer would share losses or provide liquidity that must later be repaid. That distinction matters. A liquidity facility can help a guarantee scheme meet payments during a temporary shortage of cash, but it does not necessarily absorb the ultimate cost of a bank failure. A loss-sharing mechanism would create a deeper form of mutual support and would be politically harder to agree.

The wider competitiveness problem

The debate is taking place as European banks face pressure to invest in technology, cybersecurity and artificial intelligence while competing with larger US institutions and non-bank lenders, a shift explored in TTN Finance’s guide to private credit and shadow banking. Fragmented national rules on consumer protection, customer onboarding, taxation and insolvency can make it expensive for a bank to expand across the bloc. EDIS was designed to shield national guarantee systems from large local shocks and weaken the link between banks and their home sovereigns. Any alternative would need to preserve that goal.

What happens next

The discussions are exploratory. No final design, legislative timetable or agreement among member states has been announced. The next test will be whether the Commission can turn the options into a proposal that addresses cross-border banking barriers without reopening the same argument over who ultimately bears the cost of failures.

For depositors, the existing €100,000 national guarantee remains the relevant protection. For banks, the outcome could influence how easily they can organise subsidiaries, branches, capital and liquidity across the single market. The difference between a temporary liquidity backstop and genuine European loss-sharing will determine whether the new approach merely improves coordination or materially changes the safety net.
