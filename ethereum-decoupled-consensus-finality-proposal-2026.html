Ethereum’s New Consensus Draft Targets a Fourfold Cut in Finality Time

A new Ethereum draft separates two validator voting jobs to pursue faster finality. It remains unfinished and has no activation date.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-22

Ethereum researchers have submitted a draft that seeks to reduce the time needed for transactions to become final by at least fourfold. The proposal, created October 21 and reported by CoinDesk on October 22, would separate two voting duties currently linked in Ethereum’s consensus system. It is a design proposal, not a live network upgrade.

The initial ambition translates roughly into moving from today’s approximately 16-minute finality interval toward four minutes, subject to the parameters developers ultimately select. The distinction matters for readers moving assets between networks: a transaction appearing in a block is not the same as the network giving it the stronger settlement guarantee known as finality.

Two voting jobs, on different schedules

The draft, titled Decoupled Consensus and numbered EIP-8453 in its working branch, was authored by Francesco D’Amato, Roberto Saltini, Yann Vonlanthen, Mikhail Kalinin and Ben Edgington. Its core change is to separate votes that help choose the chain’s current head from attestations that establish finality. The finality process could then run on its own schedule.

The authors want the design to improve resilience against reorganizations, shorten settlement delays and create a foundation for a possible later transition to post-quantum cryptography. The proposal does not select a quantum-resistant signature scheme. Nor does it say that existing Ethereum transactions become quantum resistant merely because the two voting processes are separated.

The working design uses a smaller committee for head votes and the full validator set for finality attestations. Finalization relies on quorums representing at least two-thirds of active stake. Important choices remain unresolved, including timing, penalties, integration details and parts of the security analysis. The draft explicitly calls for further executable tests.

Why a few minutes can matter financially

A cross-chain service may provide funds to a customer before the original transaction is fully settled. That can improve the customer’s experience, but leaves the service bearing exposure while it waits. Reducing that interval can let the same pool of capital support more transfers, provided the security assumptions remain acceptable to the service.

Consider an illustrative operation that advances $600,000 an hour at a steady rate. A 16-minute settlement wait would leave about $160,000 awaiting completion; a four-minute wait would reduce that amount to about $40,000. This is a simple flow-times-delay calculation, not an observed Ethereum business result. It excludes reserves for volatility, operational failures and uneven customer demand.

The mechanism is relevant to stablecoin transfers, where users may care more about reliable dollar delivery than the price of the network’s token. Circle’s Cross-Chain Transfer Protocol documentation, for example, distinguishes messages handled at different finality thresholds. Application designers already make explicit decisions about how much settlement assurance a transfer requires.

Faster base-layer finality could alter those decisions, but would not automatically lower every customer fee. A provider’s pricing also reflects infrastructure costs, available liquidity, its risk policy and competition. Nor would a shorter finality interval by itself reduce the gas required to execute a transaction. Settlement confidence and transaction-processing costs are related business concerns, not interchangeable technical measurements.

A hard fork would require considerably more work

The specification says implementation would require a consensus-layer hard fork and support from consensus and validator clients. Transition rules must preserve accountability for earlier votes. The authors also identify unresolved questions about recovery after network partitions, validator participation and processing limits. Those are central requirements for evaluating whether a faster design remains robust under stress.

For readers of our DeFi guide, the practical implication is that applications would still need to assess the security of the complete transaction path. A faster source chain cannot remove an unrelated smart-contract vulnerability, destination-network failure or custody risk. Developers would have to evaluate the proposed guarantees rather than substitute a headline speed figure for a risk assessment.

The proposal adds a concrete new direction to Ethereum’s research pipeline without committing the network to a deployment date. The milestones to watch are a complete specification, security analysis, interoperable client implementations, testing and an agreed upgrade schedule. Until those steps occur, users should continue to follow the confirmation requirements of the services they actually use.
