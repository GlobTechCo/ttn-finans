UK Inflation Reaches 3.5% as Public Debt Passes £3 Trillion

September figures show broader price pressures and higher debt-interest costs, complicating the choices facing monetary and fiscal policymakers.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-21

UK consumer-price inflation accelerated to 3.5% in September from 3.1% in August, while public-sector net debt provisionally crossed £3 trillion, official figures released Wednesday showed. Together, the reports expose the pressure that higher prices place on both household spending power and government finances.

The Office for National Statistics said motor fuels were the largest upward driver of the change in annual inflation. But measures excluding volatile items also increased, making the report more consequential than a rise confined to petrol prices.

Underlying measures also move higher

Core CPI, which excludes energy, food, alcohol and tobacco, rose 2.8% over the year, up from 2.6%. CPI services inflation increased to 3.7% from 3.4%. Headline CPI increased 0.4% during September itself, compared with little change in the same month last year.

Those comparisons describe different things. The monthly figure measures the price change from August to September. The annual figure compares the latest basket with September 2025. Because an annual rate replaces one month in a twelve-month calculation, what happened a year earlier can change the rate even without a dramatic new monthly movement.

Core inflation helps identify pressures beyond the most volatile categories, but it does not exclude every effect of energy. Fuel and electricity can enter the costs of producing and delivering other goods and services. A rise in core inflation therefore warrants scrutiny without, on its own, proving that every business has permanently changed its pricing behavior.

The debt milestone comes with an important qualification

Public-sector net borrowing was £24.5 billion in September, £1.9 billion above the Office for Budget Responsibility’s forecast. Central-government debt interest reached £10.9 billion, £2 billion above forecast. The ONS attributed the broader borrowing pressure to spending increasing faster than receipts, including higher interest costs.

Net debt was provisionally £3,005 billion, or 94% of GDP. Although the cash amount crossed a new threshold, the ratio to economic output was 0.7 percentage points lower than a year earlier. These measures can move in different directions because the cash value of the economy changes as well as the debt stock.

Borrowing across the financial year to September was £97.8 billion, £4.4 billion below the comparable period last year but £6 billion above forecast. Both comparisons matter: an improvement against last year can still leave policymakers with less room than their budget plans assumed. The latest estimates are provisional and can be revised.

The milestone should not be read as a specific trigger for a funding crisis. A debt stock describes accumulated obligations; borrowing describes the deficit during a period. Assessing sustainability also requires financing costs, growth, maturities and the future balance between revenue and spending, as explained in the relationship between interest rates, growth and debt.

An energy shock creates a difficult policy choice

Bank of England Governor Andrew Bailey set out the underlying dilemma in a May speech. Monetary policy cannot remove the initial loss of purchasing power caused by more expensive imported energy. Its role is to prevent the adjustment from becoming persistent inflation, while recognizing that interest-rate changes affect demand with a delay.

The distinction between direct and second-round effects matters here. A direct effect is the price of fuel itself. A second-round effect can arise when expectations of continuing inflation alter wage bargaining and businesses’ subsequent prices. The September core and services readings add information, but they do not alone establish the strength or duration of that process.

The Bank’s explanation of its remit also recognizes a trade-off between bringing inflation back to target and unnecessary volatility in output. Tightening too little risks persistent price pressure; tightening too strongly can weaken activity after the initial energy shock has faded. Wednesday’s statistics inform that judgment rather than predetermine a policy vote.

For U.S. investors, the combination is relevant to sterling assets, British government bonds and companies with UK customers. Higher expected rates can support a currency through yield differences, while weaker spending prospects can weigh on earnings. Neither channel guarantees a particular market reaction. The next policy decisions will also depend on subsequent evidence about wages, demand and the persistence of price increases.
