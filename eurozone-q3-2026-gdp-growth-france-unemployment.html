Eurozone Growth Beats Forecasts, but France Contracts and Unemployment Rises

Eurozone GDP grew 0.4% in the third quarter. A French contraction, rising unemployment counts and higher inflation forecasts complicate the outlook.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-30

The eurozone economy grew twice as fast as economists expected in the third quarter, but Friday’s data exposed a less comfortable picture beneath the headline. France contracted, the number of unemployed people increased, and professional forecasters raised their inflation estimates. The combination leaves investors weighing resilient output against pressure on household budgets and financing costs.

Gross domestic product increased 0.4% from the preceding quarter, Eurostat’s preliminary estimate showed. Economists in a Reuters poll had expected 0.2%. The expansion was slower than the second quarter’s 0.6% increase, so the report represents an upside surprise alongside a loss of momentum. Both comparisons are necessary to understand what changed.

The figures measure inflation-adjusted output. A positive real growth rate means production expanded after accounting for price changes, an important distinction when energy is becoming more expensive. Yet the aggregate covers economies with different industrial structures, public finances and demand conditions. A stronger regional result can coexist with weakening activity in a major member country.

The national picture is uneven

France’s output declined 0.1% from the previous quarter. Germany grew 0.2%, Spain and the Netherlands each expanded 0.6%, and Italy was unchanged. The eurozone’s year-over-year growth rate reached 1.3%. These figures show the difference between the direction of output in the latest quarter and its level relative to a year earlier.

Ireland reported a 5.5% quarterly increase, the strongest in the available national data. Eurostat labels the Irish flash estimate experimental and urges particular care in interpreting it. A large percentage move in one economy cannot simply be added to the rates in other countries; its contribution to the regional result depends on its economic weight.

For companies selling across Europe, that dispersion affects the usefulness of a single regional forecast. A business concentrated in French customers can face a different demand environment from one concentrated in Spain. Investors examining sales guidance therefore need to connect the geographic distribution of revenue with local activity, rather than applying the eurozone average to every operation.

A stable jobless rate masks a larger unemployment count

September’s eurozone unemployment rate held at 6.5%, unchanged from August but above 6.3% a year earlier, according to a separate Eurostat release. The number of unemployed people rose by 122,000 during the month to 11.555 million. The unchanged rounded rate and the increase in people out of work are compatible measures.

A rate divides the unemployed population by the labor force, while a headcount records the number of people meeting the definition. Changes in that denominator and rounding can make the measures move differently. Eurostat’s definition covers people without a job who recently sought work and are available to start, rather than everyone outside paid employment.

The younger workforce faced a sharper deterioration. The eurozone unemployment rate for people under 25 rose to 15.4% from 15.1% in August. This rate refers to young people in the labor force, so it should not be read as the proportion of every young resident who lacks a job. Students outside the labor force are treated differently.

Inflation expectations complicate the growth signal

The European Central Bank’s latest Survey of Professional Forecasters put average inflation at 3.0% for 2026 and 2.6% for 2027, upward revisions of 0.3 and 0.4 percentage points. Respondents principally cited more persistent energy pressures. The survey is a record of outside forecasters’ expectations, distinct from an official ECB interest-rate decision or a newly measured inflation rate.

The timing also matters. The survey collected 52 responses between October 2 and October 7, before Friday’s GDP release. Its forecasts therefore reflect the information available during that earlier window. Publishing the survey and the output estimate on the same day does not mean the forecasts already incorporate every detail of the new report.

Higher energy costs can spread through transport, manufacturing and service bills. A separate question is whether workers and businesses seek compensation through wages and prices, creating more persistent second-round effects. Forecasters still expect those effects to be limited. Their assessment leaves room for continued output growth while recognizing pressure on purchasing power and margins.

What US investors can take from the release

American investors encounter the region through multinational earnings, European securities and the EUR/USD exchange rate. Currency translation can change the dollar value of European sales even when the underlying local-currency business is stable. The economic report provides information about activity; the eventual currency response also depends on interest-rate expectations and developments in the United States.

TTN’s latest US growth report offers a useful comparison, provided the reporting conventions are aligned: US headline quarterly GDP growth is annualized, while the European 0.4% figure is a direct quarter-to-quarter change. Eurostat will publish its next estimate on November 13. That update will help establish how much of Friday’s resilience survives fuller information.
