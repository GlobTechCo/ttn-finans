Bank of England Warns AI Financing Boom Is Deepening Risks Across Financial Markets

The Bank of England says opaque AI funding could spread losses across markets if expected technology returns disappoint.

The Bank of England warned on November 26 that the financing behind the artificial-intelligence infrastructure boom is becoming more complex, interconnected and difficult to assess. In its November Financial Stability Report, the central bank said AI investment has supported global growth, but the rapid expansion of funding through bonds, private credit, leveraged finance and off-balance-sheet arrangements is increasing the likelihood that stress in one part of the system could spread elsewhere.

The warning is not a claim that an AI-related financial crisis is imminent. The Bank’s Financial Policy Committee said spillovers between markets had so far been limited and that the UK financial system remained resilient. Its concern is that the scale of financing is rising faster than transparency about where the ultimate risks sit. Related context: The AI Spending Debate Is About Returns on Capital

AI borrowing is moving beyond corporate bonds

The Bank of England expects bond issuance by large technology companies known as hyperscalers to exceed $305 billion in 2026, equal to around 8% of global investment-grade bond issuance. The Bank said the share rises considerably when the broader AI-related issuer ecosystem, including semiconductor companies and data-centre operators, is counted.

Borrowing is also spreading across a wider range of financing channels. Companies are using investment-grade bonds, leveraged loans, private credit, public equity and private equity to fund data centres, chips and computing capacity. This broad funding base allows projects to proceed at a scale that would be difficult to support through operating cash flow alone. It also links technology companies to a growing number of banks, institutional investors, private-credit funds and insurers.

The Bank said borrowing by hyperscalers, data-centre operators and other AI infrastructure firms was almost double its 2025 level. It also highlighted the increasing role of private markets, where limited disclosure can make it difficult to establish the size and distribution of exposures.

Why circular financing matters

A central concern is the use of arrangements in which companies are simultaneously investors, customers and suppliers. A large cloud provider may invest in an AI company, agree to purchase its services and commit to using infrastructure built by a separate data-centre operator. A chip designer may also provide financing to customers that buy its products. Related context: Data Centers Face a Power Constraint as Well as a Chip Constraint

These relationships can support investment and create commercial value. Long-term purchase commitments give infrastructure developers more predictable revenue, which can help them secure loans or issue debt. But the same structure can make demand and future cash flows appear more secure than they would be if the companies were financially independent.

The Bank described such arrangements as circular financing. It warned that leases, purchase agreements, guarantees and other off-balance-sheet commitments can make it harder to identify potential liabilities and understand which institutions ultimately bear the risk.

The central bank’s internal estimate puts hyperscalers’ total potential exposure, including debt and off-balance-sheet contractual commitments, at just over $4 trillion. That is not a measure of current losses or a forecast that the entire amount will become debt. It illustrates the scale of commitments that may become relevant if expected AI revenues fall short or projects cannot generate sufficient cash flow.

Private credit adds another layer

Private credit has become an important source of funding for data centres and other AI-related infrastructure. The Bank cited estimates from Morgan Stanley analysts that $700 billion of data-centre capital expenditure between 2026 and 2028 could be financed by private credit. J.P. Morgan analysts separately estimated that $1.4 trillion of anticipated AI capital expenditure could be financed by alternative capital. Those are external estimates, not forecasts produced by the Bank of England, and they are not directly comparable.

The concern is not that private credit is inherently unsuitable for infrastructure. Long-term projects can match the investment horizon of private funds. The challenge is that private loans are less transparent than publicly traded bonds, and complex structures can make it harder for investors and supervisors to see concentration risks.

What investors should watch

The Bank of England’s warning shifts attention from the headline size of AI capital expenditure to the way that investment is financed. Revenue growth and chip demand remain important, but investors also need to assess leverage, contractual commitments, customer concentration, refinancing needs and the quality of cash flows behind data-centre projects.

A downturn in AI spending would not affect every company equally. Established technology groups may be better positioned than highly leveraged infrastructure developers, although circular arrangements can create indirect exposures across the sector.

For now, the Bank’s assessment is one of rising vulnerability rather than immediate systemic distress. The AI buildout is supporting economic activity, but the financial architecture behind it is becoming more interconnected. If returns from AI infrastructure eventually justify the investment, much of the borrowing may be absorbed by future cash flows. If they do not, the complexity of the funding network could make losses harder to locate and contain.

The next phase of the AI cycle will therefore be judged not only by how much computing capacity companies build, but by whether the revenues generated by that capacity can support the capital and debt committed to it.
