XRP Ledger Prepares Token-Permission Fix After Validator Delay

An amendment expected as early as November 4 aims to stop accounts allowed to destroy issued tokens from creating new ones. The issue concerns tokens built on XRPL, not the supply of XRP itself.

TTN Research Desk2026-11-02

The XRP Ledger is approaching a security-related protocol change designed to close an unusual gap between two powers that token issuers normally keep separate: the ability to destroy issued tokens and the authority to create them. As of November 2, an amendment known as fixCleanup3_4_0 was expected to become eligible for activation on November 4, subject to continued validator support and the network's approval process.

The distinction matters because the XRP Ledger supports assets issued by businesses and other organizations, including stablecoins. An issuer might authorize a payment processor to remove tokens from circulation after users redeem them for conventional money. That limited operational role should not automatically grant the processor permission to increase the outstanding supply.

According to CoinDesk's reporting and the network's technical guidance, a flaw could allow an account holding a token-destruction permission to create issued tokens under certain conditions. The planned change is intended to enforce the separation between those functions. It does not imply that someone has exploited the loophole, and it does not enable the creation of new native XRP.

Why minting and burning need separate authorization

Issued tokens are claims or digital representations whose properties depend on the entity responsible for them. For a stablecoin, the issuing organization may promise redemption under specified terms. A token's on-chain supply therefore needs to align with the issuer's authorized creation and redemption processes.

Burning typically removes units from circulation. Minting increases units in circulation. Allowing the same account to perform both operations may be appropriate in a tightly controlled system, but granting only the narrower permission is a common way to limit operational risk. A processor handling redemptions may need to destroy redeemed units without gaining a broad right to issue fresh units.

When software interprets a permission too broadly, the problem is not merely cosmetic. Businesses must be able to rely on authorization boundaries when moving value between customers, custodians and issuers. A loophole at the protocol level can weaken assumptions on which operational controls have been built, even before there is evidence of actual harm.

That does not mean all tokens issued on XRPL are automatically compromised. The exposure depends on how permissions were configured and which accounts were entrusted with particular functions. Issuers and infrastructure providers need to assess those configurations rather than assume the flaw affected every asset equally.

Network governance explains the delay

Protocol changes on XRPL are not activated simply because a development team announces them. Amendments need sustained backing from validators, the independent participants that help agree on the network's transaction history. The threshold is more than 80% support for two uninterrupted weeks.

CoinDesk reported support from 33 of 35 tracked trusted validators on November 2. With that denominator, at least 29 must continue backing the amendment for the clock to complete. A temporary decline to 26 validators on October 21 interrupted the previous approval period, delaying the expected activation from October 23 to early November. Support later recovered, but the required continuous approval period had to begin again.

The earliest reported activation window was around 14:46 UTC on November 4, provided support remained above the threshold and the network completed its activation checkpoint. That is a projected window, not confirmation the change has gone live. Coverage published before that moment should describe the fix as expected or pending.

This process can be frustrating when developers want a security change deployed quickly. It also demonstrates an important feature of open blockchain governance: rule changes require agreement across a distributed validator community. The resulting timetable is determined by the protocol's voting mechanics, not by a single company's release schedule.

Other changes are included in the amendment

The same package addresses calculations involving trading pools, vault deposits and withdrawals, and loan-related records. Some of these systems are components of broader lending functionality that has not yet received the validator support needed for its own launch. A correction in a software package should not be mistaken for activation of every associated financial product.

That distinction is especially important in cryptocurrency coverage, where headlines sometimes group together proposed upgrades, approved amendments and already-active network features. Each has a different operational meaning for users and businesses. Technical availability does not necessarily imply commercial readiness or regulatory approval.

For stablecoin issuers, the near-term practical question is whether permission configurations should be changed before or after the protocol update. Users who simply hold XRP do not need to infer from this story that the native token's supply mechanism has changed. XRP's issuance structure is separate from the permissions used for assets created by third parties on its ledger.

The broader lesson for tokenized finance

As financial firms experiment with tokenized deposits, stablecoins and digital securities, authorization design becomes central to trust. Firms need to know exactly which system components can create, transfer, redeem or destroy assets. Audits and operational procedures can reduce risk, but they depend on the underlying software consistently enforcing the controls they describe.

The XRP Ledger fix addresses a narrow yet consequential boundary. If it activates as planned, it should close the identified permission gap. The event will not, by itself, prove that every token issuer has sound procedures or that all vulnerabilities have been eliminated. Those judgments require separate audits, transparent incident reporting and ongoing technical review.

For now, the verified news is a pending protocol change with strong reported validator support, a documented delay and a clearly defined security purpose. Readers should watch the network's amendment status rather than treat an expected November 4 activation as an accomplished fact.
