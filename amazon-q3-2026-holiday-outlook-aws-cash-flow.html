Amazon’s Holiday Sales Outlook Tempers an AWS Growth Surge

AWS sales jumped 39%, but Amazon’s holiday revenue forecast midpoint missed expectations. Heavy AI investment also pushed annual free cash flow below zero.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-29

Amazon forecast holiday-quarter sales whose midpoint fell slightly short of Wall Street expectations Thursday, tempering a sharp acceleration in its cloud business. The results leave investors weighing stronger operating profitability against the enormous cash commitment required to expand artificial-intelligence infrastructure.

The company projected fourth-quarter revenue of $240 billion to $247 billion. The $243.5 billion midpoint was about $1 billion below the $244.51 billion analyst estimate compiled by LSEG and reported by Reuters. The upper end exceeded that estimate, so the guidance was not uniformly below expectations. Amazon shares fell in after-hours trading, Reuters reported.

That distinction matters when interpreting a forecast range. A midpoint gives investors a convenient comparison with consensus, but it does not specify where management expects the final result to land. Thursday’s announcement describes several possible outcomes, including one above the analyst benchmark, rather than a confirmed holiday sales shortfall.

Cloud growth carries a larger profit contribution

Amazon’s third-quarter sales reached $201.9 billion, up 12% from a year earlier. Amazon Web Services, the division selling computing infrastructure and related services, generated $46 billion in revenue, up roughly 39%. Its operating income reached $17.8 billion, compared with $11.4 billion a year earlier, according to the company’s results.

Those figures put AWS at approximately 23% of group sales but about 63% of group operating income, calculations based on the reported totals. The relationship helps explain why cloud performance can dominate the investment debate even when shoppers and delivery vans remain the company’s most visible business.

It also means a dollar of additional sales does not have the same financial significance across Amazon’s operations. A change in the mix between cloud services and retail can alter consolidated profitability even if total revenue growth barely changes. Reading only the group sales figure misses that difference between the businesses.

The broader question resembles the one raised in Microsoft’s latest cloud and cash-flow results: whether demand for computing services is translating into financial returns quickly enough to support the infrastructure being built. Rising sales establish customer activity; they do not, by themselves, establish the return on each new facility.

Record operating margin, negative free cash flow

Amazon reported operating income of $28.1 billion and a 13.9% operating margin, which it described as a company record. Yet free cash flow over the trailing twelve months was negative $42.4 billion, compared with positive $14.8 billion a year earlier. The company attributed the deterioration primarily to higher property and equipment spending, largely for AI.

These measures answer different questions. Operating profit spreads the accounting cost of long-lived equipment across its useful life. Cash flow records money leaving the business when investments are paid for. Expanding infrastructure can therefore coincide with improving operating earnings and a weaker cash balance after investment.

The negative figure covers twelve months, not just the third quarter. It should not be described as a three-month cash loss. Nor does negative free cash flow mean the underlying operations stopped generating money. TTN’s guide to free cash flow explains why the spending deducted from operating cash is central to interpreting this measure.

Amazon maintained its approximately $220 billion forecast for 2026 cash capital expenditures. That full-year projection and the trailing-twelve-month free-cash-flow calculation cover different periods and definitions. Subtracting one from the other would not produce a meaningful estimate of cash remaining at year-end.

The holiday test extends beyond a headline beat

For investors, the next quarter tests two connected propositions: that cloud expansion can sustain profitable demand, and that the wider company can deliver the revenue implied by its holiday outlook. Stronger performance in one division can offset weakness elsewhere, but it does not eliminate the cash obligations of the group.

Thursday’s release illustrates why an earnings assessment requires more than a beat-or-miss label. The forecast comparison is relatively narrow, while the difference between operating profitability and cash investment is much larger. Subsequent revenue, margins and cash spending will show whether those measures move closer together as the new capacity becomes productive.
