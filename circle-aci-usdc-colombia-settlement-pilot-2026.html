Circle and ACI Plan a USDC Settlement Pilot in Colombia

The initiative tests stablecoin settlement alongside existing banking infrastructure. It does not announce a nationwide USDC rollout or replace Colombia’s peso payments system.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-27

Circle and payments-software company ACI Worldwide announced a collaboration on Tuesday to demonstrate USDC settlement inside existing banking infrastructure, with Colombia as the initial focus. The initiative addresses a less visible part of digital payments: how institutions complete the movement of value after a customer or business starts a transfer.

The companies plan to use ACI Connetic, a platform that coordinates different payment systems, together with Circle's USDC infrastructure. ACI built the central infrastructure behind Bre-B, Colombia's interoperable instant-payments ecosystem. The announcement describes a pilot alongside that established infrastructure. It does not say that Bre-B has switched to USDC or that every participating bank is offering stablecoin accounts.

This distinction matters because a dollar-linked token and a domestic account transfer solve different problems. USDC represents a claim intended to track the U.S. dollar, while a local payment system connects accounts and institutions. Our stablecoin explainer examines why the asset's backing, redemption arrangements and access to liquidity remain important even when its transfer takes place quickly.

A payment instruction is only part of a payment

An institution connecting stablecoins to its existing operations must track more than a transaction identifier. It needs to associate the transfer with the right customer or obligation, decide when the payment is considered complete, and reconcile the resulting balances. The operational record must remain understandable when one part of a journey uses a bank account and another uses a blockchain.

Consider a business that needs to pay an invoice denominated in pesos but holds dollar-linked tokens. A token transfer alone does not establish the exchange rate, identify the provider of pesos or guarantee that the recipient can spend the local currency immediately. These are examples of implementation questions, not features that Tuesday's pilot announcement says have already been solved.

ACI and Circle describe their work as helping institutions evaluate settlement within familiar operating environments. The release does not disclose transaction volumes, participating financial institutions, a production launch date or measured customer savings. The relevant evidence will be how a live implementation handles the full journey, including conversion, controls, reconciliation and exceptions, rather than simply whether a token can be sent.

Dollar reserves and local liquidity are separate issues

Circle describes USDC as redeemable one-for-one for U.S. dollars. Its transparency materials describe reserves that include bank deposits, short-term Treasuries and overnight Treasury repurchase agreements. That framework concerns the backing of the token. It does not mean a Colombian payment recipient automatically has direct access to Circle's redemption service or receives pesos without an intermediary.

The difference resembles the distinction between owning an asset and having access to the cash needed for a particular payment. An institution may need liquidity in both currencies, contractual arrangements with service providers and procedures for handling a delay. A dollar redemption promise does not fix the dollar-peso exchange rate or eliminate the cost of moving between financial systems.

Our coverage of stablecoin distribution economics shows why access and commercial arrangements matter alongside token issuance. A broader payments footprint can create opportunities for an issuer, but an announced integration does not establish new revenue, profitable transaction volumes or the share of any fees retained by the companies involved.

The pilot tests integration rather than a new currency regime

The companies say Connetic supports traditional payments, card activity and digital assets through one orchestration platform. That is a proposed way to coordinate routes, not evidence that all those routes share the same legal treatment, settlement finality or hours of cash redemption. A useful pilot would make those differences manageable within the institution's actual operations.

The announcement also leaves room for other networks and future settlement models. It should therefore be read as an integration initiative rather than an exclusive conversion of Colombia's financial system to one token. Our guide to blockchain financial infrastructure explains why moving instructions into software does not remove the surrounding custody and governance decisions. For Circle and ACI, the next meaningful milestone is evidence from implementation: which institutions participate, what goes live, and what costs or operational problems the system demonstrably resolves.
