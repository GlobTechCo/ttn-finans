Crypto Industry Enters US Midterms After $176 Million Political Spending Push

Crypto groups have spent about $176 million to influence US politics, Reuters found. With the midterms approaching, the stakes extend well beyond campaign donations to the rules governing digital assets.

TTN Research Desk2026-11-02

Cryptocurrency groups have poured about $176 million into US political campaigns, according to Reuters. Their spending puts digital-asset regulation in focus ahead of the November 3 midterm elections. A Reuters investigation published in late October found that industry groups had spent approximately $176 million across more than 100 congressional races during the election cycle. As voting approaches, the central question for digital-asset businesses is not simply which party wins. It is whether the next Congress creates durable rules for trading platforms, stablecoins and the treatment of digital assets.

What the spending figure means

Reuters reported that the campaign spans multiple political organisations, including the large crypto-focused super PAC Fairshake and its affiliates. The $176 million figure covers election-cycle spending identified in the investigation; it is not a new amount spent on November 2. The timing of this analysis is the election eve, while the underlying campaign-finance reporting predates it.

According to Reuters, Fairshake and affiliates had spent about $150.8 million through October 20, with additional crypto organisations contributing roughly $25 million. The industry has supported candidates across party lines, favouring politicians considered receptive to its regulatory goals. This strategy reflects a longer-term effort to develop influence in Congress rather than a single election-day bet.

Super PACs can accept large donations and make independent political expenditures under US rules, but they cannot coordinate their spending directly with a candidate's campaign. Their advertisements often focus on general political issues rather than cryptocurrency. This matters because a viewer may not understand that financial and technology interests are financing a message that appears unrelated to digital-asset regulation.

A strategic change for the industry

Cryptocurrency companies once often positioned themselves as outsiders to the traditional financial system. Their political activity now resembles the strategies of established industries that seek predictable legislation and a say in regulatory design. Reuters identified businesses and investors associated with Coinbase, Ripple and Andreessen Horowitz among significant sources of financial support for the wider effort.

The industry's case is that clear rules could encourage investment, improve consumer protection and reduce uncertainty about legal responsibilities. Critics argue that spending on this scale risks giving wealthy market participants disproportionate influence over laws meant to govern their activities. Both concerns can be valid subjects of public scrutiny. Campaign donations do not establish that any particular future law is appropriate or that a candidate will vote in a specified way.

A key distinction is between regulation that creates meaningful safeguards and regulation that simply makes business easier. Market participants may want consistent standards for custody, token classification and exchange registration. Consumers also need credible controls over conflicts of interest, financial disclosures, cyber resilience and the handling of customer assets. Rules that are clear but weak would not necessarily make the market safer.

Why Congress matters to investors

The executive branch and federal regulators play a large role in supervising digital assets, but legislation can establish the longer-term boundaries of those powers. Congressional committees influence financial-market structure, bank participation, oversight budgets and enforcement priorities. Even when the presidency remains unchanged, shifts in either chamber can alter what legislation is possible.

Investors may respond to election outcomes by reassessing the probability of new legislation. However, an election result is not a regulation, and a bill passing one chamber is not equivalent to becoming law. The details of committee control, legislative priorities and party divisions may matter more than a simple headline about which party wins.

What the election cannot immediately change

The November 3 vote will not determine the price of Bitcoin or Ether on its own. Cryptocurrency valuations also reflect interest rates, liquidity, leverage, macroeconomic conditions, technical risks and investor sentiment. Companies might welcome an election outcome perceived as supportive while token prices fall for unrelated reasons.

Reuters reported that 53 of the 58 candidates supported by Fairshake in primaries or special elections advanced to the November ballot. That figure shows the PAC's ability to select and support viable candidates; it does not reveal what proportion of those victories was caused by its spending. Separating correlation from causation is essential in evaluating corporate political influence.

The transparency question

Advocacy groups, including Public Citizen, have criticised the scale of corporate election expenditure. The underlying concern extends beyond cryptocurrency: voters need to know who is financing advertisements and what policy objectives donors may pursue afterward. Financial disclosures and independent monitoring provide a basis for scrutiny, but the organisations' strategies can still be difficult for ordinary voters to track.

What to watch after November 3

The first useful indicators will be the composition of the House and Senate, leadership of relevant committees and statements by legislators about financial-market priorities. Subsequently, investors should track the introduction of specific bills, their consumer-protection provisions, committee hearings and any realistic timetable for passage.

A larger bipartisan coalition could make durable digital-asset legislation more plausible. Divided government or competing policy priorities could delay action. Neither outcome should be sold as certain before votes are counted and legislative negotiations begin. The immediate story is the scale of political spending; the longer-term story is whether voters and lawmakers can turn a contentious industry debate into clear, credible rules.
