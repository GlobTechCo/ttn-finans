Bitcoin ETF Outflows Reach $3.47 Billion Through Nov. 19

Bitcoin ETF redemptions rose in November as CFTC staff clarified how regulated derivatives firms may use crypto assets.

TTN Research DeskNov 25, 2026 · 3:25 AM EST

Bitcoin’s institutional demand is under pressure as US spot exchange-traded funds record sustained net withdrawals. The latest complete daily figures displayed by Farside Investors show $3.47 billion in net outflows from November 2 through November 19. The total is a calculation from the provider’s daily figures, not a published monthly total, and it deliberately excludes later dates for which the table does not show complete data.

The three sessions from November 17 to November 19 produced combined net outflows of $1.13 billion. Daily withdrawals were $239.9 million on November 17, $306.5 million on November 18 and $582.3 million on November 19. On the final day, BlackRock’s iShares Bitcoin Trust (IBIT) recorded a $541.1 million outflow, Fidelity’s FBTC lost $45.9 million and Morgan Stanley’s MSBT recorded a $49.6 million inflow. These are fund-level net flows, not a record of every institutional bitcoin trade.

Adding Farside’s daily totals from November 2 through November 19 gives net redemptions of approximately $3.468 billion. The calculation includes positive-flow sessions as well as withdrawals, so it is a net figure rather than the sum of negative days alone. Farside’s displayed table contains no complete fund-level entries for November 20 and no later daily rows in the data reviewed for this update. Those missing observations are not treated as zero and are not included in the total.

Bitcoin’s market price and ETF redemptions are related but are not interchangeable measures. ETF flows record net creations and redemptions of fund shares; they do not capture every institutional purchase or sale across direct holdings, futures, offshore venues or over-the-counter markets. A falling bitcoin price also reduces the value of ETF assets even when investors do not redeem shares. Fund flows should therefore be read alongside price, trading volume and derivatives positioning rather than as a complete measure of institutional exposure.

BlackRock’s IBIT was the largest contributor to the November 19 outflow, with $541.1 million in net redemptions on that day. The concentration is relevant because IBIT is one of the largest US spot Bitcoin ETFs and a major route through which traditional investors obtain bitcoin exposure without holding the asset directly. However, one fund’s daily flow cannot establish whether all institutional investors are reducing exposure: other products recorded both withdrawals and inflows during the same session.

The figures do not prove that institutional investors have abandoned bitcoin. Net flows can reverse, and ETF activity is only one part of the market. Investors can hold bitcoin directly, use futures, trade offshore products or maintain exposure through other financial instruments. But repeated redemptions are a meaningful signal that demand through one of the most visible regulated investment channels has weakened.

A separate regulatory development in the United States points in a different direction. On November 23, staff at the Commodity Futures Trading Commission issued an advisory addressing whether bitcoin, ether and qualifying payment stablecoins can count toward certain financial-resource requirements for registered swap execution facilities, designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations. The staff said bitcoin, ether and qualifying payment stablecoins may be acceptable resources for specified operating-cost requirements, subject to valuation haircuts and liquidity conditions.

The guidance is narrower than a broad approval of crypto as regulatory capital. The advisory concerns resources used to cover operating costs, not the default resources that a clearinghouse must hold against a member failure. It requires firms to apply haircuts for market and credit risk. CFTC staff said it would not object to haircuts of at least 20% for bitcoin and ether and 2% for qualifying payment stablecoins in the circumstances described. The minimum liquid-asset buffer differs by institution: swap execution facilities must retain at least three months of unencumbered liquid assets, while designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations must retain at least six months. Crypto assets themselves do not count toward those cash or highly liquid securities requirements.

The distinction is important for market structure. The CFTC is acknowledging that certain digital assets may have a role in the financial resources of regulated derivatives businesses, but it is not treating them as equivalent to cash. Haircuts, liquidity requirements and the risk that crypto prices could fall at the same time as a firm faces operating stress remain central safeguards. The advisory is a staff position, not a rule that automatically applies to every crypto company or every token.

Together, the market and regulatory developments show two different sides of institutional adoption. Investors are withdrawing money from listed bitcoin products during a period of price weakness, while US regulators are continuing to define how digital assets can be used inside parts of the regulated financial system. One does not cancel out the other: product flows reflect investor positioning, while the CFTC advisory concerns the operational resources of specific market institutions.

For bitcoin investors, the near-term question is whether ETF redemptions slow as prices stabilize or continue to reinforce selling pressure. For the industry, the longer-term question is whether clearer rules can support more institutional infrastructure without weakening liquidity and risk controls. November’s outflows are a warning about current demand, not a final verdict on the future of regulated crypto markets.
