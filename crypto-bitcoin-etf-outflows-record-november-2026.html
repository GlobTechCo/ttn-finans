Bitcoin ETFs Lose $4.94B in November Through Nov. 24

US spot funds logged 15 outflow sessions as CFTC staff clarified crypto collateral rules for derivatives venues.

TTN Research DeskNov 25, 2026 · 3:25 AM EST

US spot Bitcoin exchange-traded funds recorded approximately $4.938 billion in net outflows across 17 trading sessions from November 2 through November 24, according to Farside Investors’ daily fund-flow data. The total is calculated by adding the published daily net figures, including sessions when money entered the funds. Fifteen sessions recorded outflows and two recorded inflows. The withdrawals make ETF demand an important market signal, but the data describe one investment channel. They do not capture every institutional bitcoin transaction or establish that all large investors have reduced exposure.

The month began with withdrawals. Farside recorded net outflows of $398.6 million on November 2 and $115.6 million on November 3, followed by a $74.6 million inflow on November 4. Another $430.2 million left the funds on November 5. That positive session matters because a month-to-date figure is a net calculation, not the sum of negative days alone. Individual products can also move in opposite directions on the same day, so an aggregate total should not be mistaken for a uniform decision by every fund investor.

Withdrawals accelerated in the latest sessions

The pressure remained heavy in the second half of the month. The funds recorded net withdrawals of $582.3 million on November 19, $669.2 million on November 20, $569.7 million on November 23 and $231.4 million on November 24. November 20 was the largest single-day outflow in the period. The four sessions together accounted for more than $2 billion in net withdrawals, showing that the pressure did not end with the November 19 move. The updated total through November 24 is materially larger than the $3.47 billion figure that covered November 2–19.

BlackRock’s iShares Bitcoin Trust, IBIT, was the largest source of cumulative withdrawals. Adding its daily figures for the 17 sessions gives net outflows of about $4.17 billion. On November 20 alone, IBIT recorded a $544.7 million outflow, while Fidelity’s FBTC lost $72.4 million. Other funds also recorded withdrawals, although some products had inflows on individual days. On November 4, for example, FBTC received $236.7 million while IBIT recorded a $201.2 million outflow. Fund-level differences are one reason to examine the daily table rather than treating the aggregate as a single investor vote.

ETF flows measure net creations and redemptions of fund shares. They are not a complete ledger of institutional bitcoin exposure. Investors can hold bitcoin directly, use futures, trade through offshore venues or obtain exposure through other financial products. A fall in assets under management can also reflect a lower bitcoin price, not only investor withdrawals. Conversely, a redemption does not reveal whether an investor sold bitcoin elsewhere or shifted to a different product. TTN’s explanation of ETF creation and redemption sets out the mechanics, while its guide to Bitcoin ETFs and institutional adoption explains what these products reveal about ownership.

A separate CFTC advisory addresses market infrastructure

On November 23, staff at the Commodity Futures Trading Commission issued Letter No. 26-31 on the use of certain crypto assets to meet operating-cost financial-resource requirements at swap execution facilities, designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations. The advisory says staff would not object to bitcoin, ether and qualifying payment stablecoins being counted for specified requirements, subject to valuation haircuts and liquidity safeguards. It does not concern the default resources a clearinghouse must hold against a clearing-member failure, and it does not change the rules governing spot Bitcoin ETFs.

Staff said it would not object to minimum haircuts of 20% for bitcoin and ether and 2% for qualifying payment stablecoins. A crypto asset that is not readily marketable and is not a qualifying payment stablecoin would generally receive a 100% haircut, meaning it could not count toward the relevant requirement. Each entity remains subject to a one-year operating-cost financial-resource requirement. For SEFs, at least the greater of three months of projected operating costs or projected wind-down costs must be held in unencumbered liquid assets; DCMs and DCOs must maintain at least six months of operating costs in such assets. Crypto assets do not qualify as cash or highly liquid securities for those minimum buffers. The staff also warns venues to consider wrong-way risk, because operational losses and crypto-price declines could coincide.

The CFTC document is a staff advisory expressing the views of the relevant divisions. It does not create new binding rules, amend existing regulations or provide blanket approval for all tokens or crypto businesses. It clarifies how specified regulated derivatives venues may treat certain digital assets within an operating-resource framework, while retaining liquidity requirements and risk haircuts. Regulatory treatment of collateral, investor demand for ETF shares and the market value of bitcoin are separate questions.

The two developments show why institutional adoption cannot be measured with one headline figure. ETF outflows indicate that investors withdrew capital from listed US spot products during the first 17 trading sessions of November. The CFTC advisory concerns the financial resources of particular derivatives-market institutions and does not explain those withdrawals. For bitcoin investors, the next useful evidence will be whether ETF flows stabilize or continue to weaken. For infrastructure providers, the question is whether the clarified framework can be applied without compromising liquidity, operational resilience or risk controls.
