Swiss Inflation Puts SNB’s Zero-Rate Policy Under Fresh Pressure

Swiss inflation reached 1.5% in October as the SNB monitored second-round effects ahead of its December policy decision.

TTN Research DeskNov 21, 2026 · 1:56 PM EST

The Swiss National Bank is facing a more difficult policy trade-off after inflation accelerated faster than expected in October and a senior policymaker warned that the central bank is monitoring the risk of second-round price effects. Vice Chairman Antoine Martin said in an interview published on November 22 that the SNB was paying particular attention to whether the recent rise in prices could become broader and more persistent. His comments do not signal that a rate increase has been decided, but they put the December policy assessment under closer scrutiny.

Switzerland’s annual consumer-price inflation rose to 1.5% in October from 1.0% in September, according to the Federal Statistical Office. The monthly increase was 0.2%. The headline rate remains inside the SNB’s 0% to 2% range for price stability, but the composition of the increase matters. Imported products were 3.7% more expensive than a year earlier, while domestic products rose 0.8%. The statistical office reported that core inflation, excluding fresh and seasonal products, energy and fuel, was 0.5%.

That gap points to an important distinction for policymakers. A rise led by imported energy can lift headline inflation without immediately creating a broad domestic wage-price cycle. But if households and businesses begin to expect higher prices to persist, workers may seek compensation and companies may pass higher costs into a wider range of goods and services. Those second-round effects are what Martin said the SNB is watching. The central bank has not said that such a process is already established. The starting point for the debate is unusually accommodative. The SNB policy rate was 0.00% on November 20, according to the central bank’s published rates. The bank left it unchanged at its September assessment, saying that medium-term inflationary pressure had increased only slightly and that policy remained appropriate. The October inflation release and Martin’s latest remarks have since added uncertainty to the next decision. A move higher is now a scenario investors must consider, not a confirmed outcome.

For bond and currency markets, the distinction between a one-off energy shock and persistent inflation is central. If the SNB concludes that higher imported prices are likely to fade, it may have room to keep rates low. If price increases broaden into services, wages and domestic costs, the case for withdrawing some accommodation would strengthen. The bank must also consider the effect of policy on the Swiss franc. A stronger franc can restrain imported inflation by reducing the local-currency cost of goods priced abroad, but it can also tighten financial conditions for exporters and tourism businesses.

Martin described the franc as a clear safe-haven currency and identified exchange-rate movements against the dollar and euro as the SNB’s main risk. The franc strengthened sharply in October amid market volatility, higher energy prices and concerns about French public debt, according to Reuters. That movement illustrates why the SNB’s decision cannot be read only through the domestic inflation number. Global risk aversion can push investors into the franc even when the central bank is not raising rates, potentially doing some of the tightening for it. The currency’s safe-haven role also complicates the outlook for Swiss companies. Exporters that earn revenue in euros or dollars may face weaker translated earnings when the franc appreciates, particularly if their costs are largely domestic. Importers and households can benefit from cheaper foreign goods and travel. A stronger currency may therefore dampen inflation while redistributing pressure across sectors. For international investors, Swiss assets can attract defensive flows during periods of uncertainty, but the exchange-rate effect can dominate the return on local equities and bonds.

The SNB’s policy choice is further constrained by the country’s low-inflation framework. At 1.5%, inflation is not outside the bank’s stated price-stability range. The core measure of 0.5% suggests that underlying domestic price pressure was still modest in October. That gives policymakers a reason to avoid reacting mechanically to one monthly report. At the same time, the rise from September was larger than expected, and the imported component was substantially stronger than domestic inflation. The bank must decide whether this is a temporary relative-price adjustment or the beginning of a broader cycle.

Investors should therefore focus on the data that can distinguish those possibilities: subsequent monthly CPI readings, core inflation, wage settlements, services prices, energy costs and the franc’s exchange rate. A single speech does not establish a policy pivot, and market expectations can change quickly as new figures arrive. The SNB’s next assessment will need to balance the risk of allowing inflation to spread against the cost of tightening too early in an economy where domestic price growth remains subdued.

For now, the message is one of vigilance rather than a promise of higher rates. The zero policy rate remains in place, and the October CPI reading is still within the SNB’s target range. But the bank has made clear that it is looking beyond the headline number. If imported inflation begins to feed into domestic prices, Switzerland’s long period of exceptionally low rates could face a more serious test.
