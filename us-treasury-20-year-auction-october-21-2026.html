US Treasury Sells $13 Billion of 20-Year Bonds at a 5.469% Yield

The reopening keeps a 5.125% coupon but sells below face value, illustrating how long-term borrowing costs differ from the Fed’s policy rate.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-21

The U.S. Treasury sold $13 billion of 20-year bonds Wednesday at a high yield of 5.469%, providing a fresh measure of the cost of financing federal debt at longer maturities. The auction reopened an existing security rather than creating a new bond with a newly set coupon. Its results show why the interest printed on a bond and the return available to a new buyer can differ.

Treasury’s October 21 results list a bid-to-cover ratio of 2.73 and a price of 95.853363 for each $100 of face value, before accrued interest. The security carries a 5.125% annual coupon, matures on August 15, 2046, and is scheduled to settle on October 23. Competitive bidding closed at 1 p.m. Eastern time.

A lower price produces a higher yield

A reopening adds to the amount outstanding of an existing security. The coupon and maturity remain attached to that bond, while bidders determine the price at which the additional supply can be sold. Wednesday’s auction therefore did not raise the coupon to 5.469%. Buyers accepted a fixed coupon at a discounted purchase price, producing the higher yield to maturity.

For $1,000 of face value, the 5.125% coupon corresponds to $51.25 of annual interest, paid in two installments. The quoted auction price equates to approximately $958.53 before accrued interest. That arithmetic describes the purchase price and contractual coupon; it does not mean an investor earns the entire difference immediately or receives a guaranteed return from selling before maturity.

Yield to maturity combines the purchase price, scheduled interest payments and eventual repayment of principal. An investor who sells earlier faces the market price available at that time. A buyer who holds the bond still needs to consider how future coupons are reinvested and what inflation does to the purchasing power of the payments received.

What the bid-to-cover ratio can establish

The reported 2.73 ratio compares submitted bids with the amount awarded. It shows that bids exceeded the available offering, but it is not a direct measure of investor confidence in the government or proof that the price was especially favorable. Bidders can submit offers at yields that Treasury does not ultimately accept, so the amount bid and the price of winning bids answer different questions.

Treasury uses a single-price auction. It accepts compliant noncompetitive bids and then competitive bids in order, beginning with the lowest yields, until the offering is allocated. Successful bidders receive the highest accepted yield. The high yield is consequently the clearing result, rather than the average of every rate investors were willing to request.

Judging whether an auction was stronger or weaker than expected also requires a comparable pre-auction market quote and information about prevailing conditions. The official result alone does not supply that benchmark. A large bid total can coexist with a clearing yield above expectations, and a smaller total can still produce an orderly sale at an anticipated price.

A different rate from the Fed’s target

A 20-year Treasury yield reflects pricing over a much longer horizon than the Federal Reserve’s overnight policy target. Expectations for inflation and future short-term rates matter, alongside the compensation investors require for holding long-duration debt and the supply available to buy. The auction does not represent a Federal Reserve decision, even when its yield changes financial conditions.

For existing bondholders, the immediate risk runs through price sensitivity. When the yield buyers demand rises, the price of a fixed stream of payments generally falls. Longer-dated securities can move substantially because more of their cash flows lie in the future. Our guide to bond duration and interest-rate sensitivity explains why a modest yield move can have a larger effect on a long bond’s market value.

The financing signal after the sale

Wednesday’s result fixes the terms of this auction, not the government’s cost of borrowing across its entire debt portfolio. Bills, notes and other bonds have different maturities and financing schedules. Nor does the sale mechanically reset mortgage rates or corporate borrowing costs, although Treasury yields help form the reference rates against which other financing is priced.

The next evidence will come from secondary-market trading and subsequent debt sales. The separate TradingView page for the 20-year Treasury yield tracks a market yield series, not a continuing auction or an offer to buy this particular bond. Comparing those later yields with the auction result can help assess how readily the newly issued supply is absorbed after settlement.
