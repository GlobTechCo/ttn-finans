Global Stocks Lose Nearly $9 Trillion as AI Valuations and Rate Fears Collide

A $9 trillion rout tests whether AI spending can deliver the returns investors have already priced in.

TTN Research DeskNov 21, 2026 · 5:57 AM EST

Global markets ended the week under heavy pressure as investors reassessed the value of technology companies and the prospect that interest rates may need to rise again. The sell-off was no longer confined to a handful of expensive artificial intelligence stocks. Equities fell across Asia, Europe and the United States, while commodities and currencies also reflected a broad retreat from risk.

Reuters reported on November 20 that almost $9 trillion had been erased from the value of global equities since markets peaked in August. Taiwan's benchmark fell 17% during the week, its steepest weekly decline since the early 1990s. Japan and South Korea each lost more than 5% on Friday, while the broad MSCI gauge of global shares recorded its largest weekly fall since early 2020.

The US market confirmed the severity of the shift. The S&P 500 dropped 3.70% on Friday to 6,910.39, putting it more than 10% below its October record and into correction territory. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1.93% to 48,147.33, also more than 10% below its high. The Nasdaq Composite declined 5.28% to 22,617.73 and turned negative for the year, according to market data reported by Reuters and the Associated Press.

Semiconductor shares were among the hardest hit. Reuters put the Friday decline in US chipmakers at 10.7%, with the group down 17.1% for the week. Investors were also reassessing the market reception for Anthropic, whose shares fell as much as 14.5% on their first full day of trading after a high-profile initial public offering. The retreat suggested that even a major listing associated with the AI boom could not insulate investors from a wider repricing.

The concern is not simply that technology shares have risen too far. Investors are questioning whether the enormous capital spending planned by cloud providers and AI developers can generate returns quickly enough to justify current valuations. Data centres require chips, power, cooling systems, land and network capacity. Those investments can support long-term demand for suppliers, but they also raise depreciation, financing and operating costs for the companies building them. TTN's analysis of data-center electricity demand explains why power access has become a central constraint on AI investment.

Interest rates have made that calculation more difficult. Reuters reported that markets were increasingly pricing the possibility of another Federal Reserve rate increase, as inflation concerns challenged expectations of easier monetary policy. Higher yields reduce the present value of future earnings, a particularly important issue for companies whose valuations depend on profits expected many years ahead. They also raise the cost of borrowing for businesses financing infrastructure and for households carrying variable-rate debt.

The bond market showed how quickly investor preferences had changed. The US 10-year Treasury yield fell by more than 10 basis points on Friday as buyers sought government debt, marking its largest daily decline in about a month. A falling yield in a sell-off can signal demand for safer assets, although it does not by itself mean that the market expects an immediate economic downturn. Investors were balancing inflation risks against the possibility that tighter financial conditions could weaken growth. TTN's report on the previous Wall Street sell-off tracks how the repricing accelerated.

The move was not uniform across assets. Gold, which often benefits from uncertainty, fell sharply and reached a 14-month low, according to Reuters. That unusual combination of falling equities and falling gold may indicate that some investors were raising cash, reducing leverage or meeting margin requirements rather than simply rotating into traditional havens. The US dollar also strengthened during the week, adding pressure to currencies and assets priced in dollars.

For companies, a prolonged repricing could affect more than share prices. A lower market valuation can make equity financing less attractive, weaken the value of stock-based compensation and raise the hurdle rate for acquisitions. Technology firms may face greater scrutiny of capital expenditure plans, while smaller suppliers could find it harder to finance expansion if customers delay projects. Banks and other lenders will also watch whether falling collateral values and higher borrowing costs affect credit quality.

For investors, the key distinction is between a correction in valuation and a deterioration in underlying business demand. AI adoption may continue to expand even if the market reduces the prices it is willing to pay for the companies supplying it. Strong earnings, cash generation and disciplined investment could help businesses withstand a reset. Companies relying on optimistic forecasts, cheap financing or continuous access to equity markets are more exposed.

The coming weeks will test whether the sell-off stabilizes as valuations adjust or deepens as earnings expectations are revised. A market rebound would not automatically resolve questions about the return on AI infrastructure spending or the inflation outlook. Equally, a sharp decline does not prove that the technology cycle has ended. What has changed is the market's tolerance for uncertainty: investors are demanding clearer evidence that future profits can support the capital being committed today.
