Entain Agrees to A$185 Million Australian AML Settlement, Pending Court Approval

Proposed penalty would settle a six-year compliance dispute, but corporate and court approvals remain outstanding.

TTN Research DeskNov 27, 2026 · 5:52 AM EST

Entain’s Australian subsidiary has reached a preliminary agreement to pay A$185 million to settle civil penalty proceedings brought by the country’s financial crime regulator, a proposed resolution that would close a costly compliance dispute but still requires approval. The London-listed betting group said on November 27 that Entain Group Pty Ltd and the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre, or AUSTRAC, had agreed in principle to resolve proceedings filed in the Federal Court in December 2024. The proposed civil penalty is A$185 million, plus AUSTRAC’s legal costs. The parties intend to submit agreed facts and admissions, while the court has been asked to vacate trial dates scheduled for the following week. The amount and admissions are not final: Entain’s board, AUSTRAC and the Federal Court must all approve the agreement. If approvals arrive on schedule, payment is expected in late December.

The case concerns alleged breaches of Australia’s Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act between December 2018 and August 2024. AUSTRAC launched proceedings in December 2024, describing the alleged failures as serious and systemic. The regulator’s case focused on weaknesses in customer due diligence, transaction monitoring, risk assessment and senior-management oversight at a business that accepted deposits around the clock through websites and apps.

AUSTRAC’s public allegations included the use of third-party channels through which cash and other deposits could be credited to betting accounts without adequate checks on the identity of the person providing the funds or their source. The regulator also alleged that Entain did not properly assess or monitor 17 higher-risk customers. Those customers collectively deposited more than A$152 million and withdrew more than A$105 million, according to AUSTRAC’s court materials. The regulator said the arrangements exposed the business and the wider financial system to the risk that criminal proceeds could be used to fund gambling. These were allegations in the proceedings; the proposed agreed statement of facts and admissions remains subject to approval.

The settlement is material for Entain because it is substantially higher than the A$100 million provision the group had previously recognised for the case. On the proposed terms, the penalty alone would exceed that provision by A$85 million, before legal costs. The difference is a direct financial charge to be reflected in the group’s accounts, although the final accounting treatment and timing will depend on the approvals and the company’s reporting. The amount also removes the uncertainty of a contested trial, which could have produced a different penalty and prolonged the legal overhang.

For investors, the distinction between a preliminary agreement and a final court order matters. The proposed settlement is not yet an enforceable final penalty. If the Federal Court does not approve the terms, the parties may have to return to litigation or negotiate a revised outcome. Entain has asked the court to vacate the trial dates, but that procedural step does not itself determine the case. The company said it had cooperated with AUSTRAC and completed a dedicated programme to improve its Australian anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing systems, processes and controls. Chief Executive Stella David described the agreement as an important step in resolving a legacy issue and said the local business now had stronger compliance arrangements and new leadership.

The wider significance extends beyond one operator. Australia has pursued several high-profile enforcement cases involving gambling companies, including penalties against casino groups. AUSTRAC’s action against Entain was notable because it targeted an online betting operator rather than a traditional casino. Digital betting platforms can process deposits and withdrawals continuously, use multiple payment channels and serve customers remotely. That combination makes effective identity verification, source-of-funds checks and monitoring essential parts of the business model, not simply back-office compliance.

The case also highlights how compliance failures can become a valuation issue. A large penalty can reduce earnings and cash available for investment, while a lengthy investigation can keep investors focused on legal and governance risks rather than operating performance. The proposed settlement may therefore remove one source of uncertainty, but it does not automatically demonstrate that all regulatory risk has disappeared. Investors will still want evidence that the upgraded controls are effective, that the business can identify suspicious activity promptly and that oversight is embedded in day-to-day operations.

Entain’s challenge is to show that the Australian operation can continue to grow without repeating the weaknesses described by the regulator. That is commercially important because the company competes in a market where customer acquisition, payments, digital engagement and regulatory permissions are closely linked. Stronger controls can add operating costs, but failures can be far more expensive through penalties, remediation, restrictions or damage to relationships with banks and payment providers. The next milestone is judicial approval of the proposed admissions and A$185 million penalty. Until that happens, the agreement should be treated as preliminary rather than a completed settlement. If approved, it would convert a long-running legal risk into a defined financial cost and allow management to focus more clearly on the performance and control environment of its Australian business.
