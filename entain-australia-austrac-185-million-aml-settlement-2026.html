Entain Agrees to A$185 Million Australian AML Settlement, Pending Court Approval

Proposed penalty would settle a six-year compliance dispute, but corporate and court approvals remain outstanding.

TTN Research DeskNov 27, 2026 · 5:52 AM EST

Entain’s Australian subsidiary has reached a preliminary agreement with the country’s financial-crime regulator to pay A$185 million to resolve civil penalty proceedings over alleged anti-money-laundering failures. The proposed amount is substantial, but the case is not yet closed: Entain’s board, AUSTRAC and the Federal Court of Australia must approve the agreement and its proposed admissions before the penalty becomes final.

The London-listed betting group disclosed the agreement on November 27. Entain Group Pty Ltd and the Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre propose to settle proceedings that began in December 2024, following alleged breaches of Australia’s Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act between December 2018 and August 2024. The parties intend to submit a Statement of Agreed Facts and Admissions. They have also asked the Federal Court to vacate the scheduled trial dates. Entain said that, subject to the required approvals, payment is expected in full in late December.

The distinction between a proposed settlement and a court order is important. Entain’s announcement says both the agreed statement and the A$185 million penalty remain subject to approval by the company’s board and AUSTRAC, as well as the court’s approval of the admissions and penalty. The court is not simply being asked to record a private commercial compromise: it must decide whether to make the proposed civil penalty orders. Until that happens, the amount and legal outcome remain provisional.

AUSTRAC’s amended court statement sets out the conduct alleged in the case. AUSTRAC alleged weaknesses in customer due diligence, transaction monitoring and the oversight of higher-risk customers. It said payment channels allowed funds to be credited to betting accounts without adequate checks on the identity of the person supplying the money or its source. The regulator also alleged that Entain failed to carry out appropriate risk-based due diligence on 17 high-risk customers. Those customers collectively deposited more than A$152 million and withdrew more than A$105 million. These figures describe allegations in the proceedings, not a finding that every dollar was criminal proceeds.

The proposed penalty is A$85 million above the A$100 million provision Entain reported for the AUSTRAC matter in its interim financial statements for the six months ended June 30, 2026. The provision reflected management’s estimate while the outcome remained uncertain. If the A$185 million penalty is approved, the company will need to account for the difference between the final liability and the amount already provided, as well as AUSTRAC’s legal costs. The precise accounting charge and cash-payment timing will depend on the final order and the group’s reporting. It would be misleading to treat the full A$185 million as a new expense on top of the existing provision.

The settlement arrives while Entain is trying to demonstrate that its Australian operation can deliver growth under tighter controls. In its October trading update, the group reported Australian online net gaming revenue up 1% year on year in the third quarter, while group net gaming revenue increased 2% on a constant-currency basis. Entain has also said it completed a dedicated programme of improvements to its Australian anti-money-laundering and counter-terrorist-financing systems. That is the company’s account of remediation; investors will want evidence that the controls work in practice, not simply confirmation that a programme has ended.

For the business, the financial penalty is only one part of the cost. Online betting operators rely on fast deposits, withdrawals, multiple payment providers and remote customer onboarding. Those features make reliable identity checks, source-of-funds controls and ongoing transaction monitoring central to the operating model. Weak controls can expose a company not only to enforcement but also to higher compliance costs, closer scrutiny from banks and payment partners, restrictions on products or markets, and reputational damage that can affect customer acquisition.

The proposed agreement may nevertheless reduce an important source of uncertainty. A contested trial could have extended the dispute and left the eventual penalty open to a court decision. A court-approved settlement would give investors a clearer estimate of the liability and allow management to focus on operating performance. It would not, however, establish that all regulatory risk has disappeared. The effectiveness of remediation, the quality of management oversight and the treatment of suspicious activity remain relevant to the company’s long-term licence to operate.

The case also sits within a wider regulatory focus on gambling-sector controls. AUSTRAC has pursued other operators over alleged failures to identify and manage money-laundering risks, reflecting the volume of funds that can move through betting platforms and related payment channels. Comparable questions about controls and financial crime have also emerged in digital-asset markets: Coinbase Ireland’s €3.25 million safeguarding penalty addressed customer-asset protections, while TRM Labs’ law-enforcement initiative focuses on tracing and freezing illicit crypto. The regulatory frameworks differ, but both show why reliable transaction records and escalation procedures matter.

The next milestone is the Federal Court’s decision on the proposed admissions and penalty. If approved, the agreement would turn a long-running legal exposure into a defined liability, with the amount exceeding the provision already recognised. Until the court rules, investors should describe the A$185 million as a proposed civil penalty, not a completed settlement.
