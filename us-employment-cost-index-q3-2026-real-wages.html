US Pay Growth Slows, but Workers Still Lose Purchasing Power

US employment costs rose 0.8% in the third quarter. Slower wage growth eases one inflation pressure, while pay still trails the rise in consumer prices.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-30

U.S. employment costs rose 0.8% in the third quarter, down from 0.9% in the previous quarter, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on October 30. Inflation-adjusted wages still declined over the year, leaving workers with weaker purchasing power despite nominal raises.

The Employment Cost Index increased 0.8% from June through September, after a 0.9% rise in the previous quarter, the Bureau of Labor Statistics said. The result matched the Econoday survey published by CME Group. It provides evidence of moderation in one important business cost, rather than proof that the broader inflation problem has ended.

A slower quarterly increase, with annual costs still rising

Civilian wages and salaries increased 0.7% during the quarter, while benefit costs rose 0.9%. Over the year, total compensation increased 3.4%, unchanged from June’s annual rate. Wage growth was 3.1%, compared with a 3.9% increase in benefits. All quarterly comparisons here are seasonally adjusted; the annual comparisons are not.

That split matters because compensation and a paycheck are different things. Employers can spend more on insurance, retirement contributions or paid leave without putting an equivalent amount of extra cash in a worker’s bank account. Benefits have economic value, but they cannot always be redirected toward rent, groceries or an unexpected repair when a household needs the money.

Nominal raises are not the same as greater buying power

The BLS reported that private-sector wages and salaries fell 0.6% over the year after adjusting for inflation. State and local government wages declined 0.5% on that basis. These measures describe changes in the index’s purchasing power, not a nominal pay cut for every employee or a statement that all households experienced identical inflation.

That distinction complements Thursday’s report on US growth and consumer spending. Output, spending and pay answer different questions. An economy can continue expanding while households face a less comfortable comparison between income and prices. Strong activity therefore does not establish that the experience of growth is equally favorable for employees, employers and investors.

Why this measure differs from the monthly wage number

The ECI is designed to track changes in hourly labor costs without the distortion caused by employment shifting among industries and occupations. A change in the mix of jobs can move a simple average even when the pay for comparable work has barely changed. Keeping that composition effect separate makes the index useful for assessing underlying compensation pressure.

Its coverage also has boundaries. The civilian measure combines private industry with state and local government, rather than representing every person who works in the United States. Federal employees, military personnel and the self-employed are outside its scope. A national figure should therefore not be read as a personalized pay benchmark for every occupation or employment arrangement.

What slower labor costs can and cannot tell the Fed

Moderating compensation can reduce one potential source of persistent service-price pressure. But the connection runs through productivity, margins and firms’ ability to pass costs to customers. Higher output per hour can absorb some labor-cost growth; weaker sales can prevent price increases. Neither relationship permits a mechanical conversion from a wage reading into a future inflation rate.

For the Federal Reserve, the useful question is whether price increases are becoming embedded in ongoing wage and pricing decisions, the mechanism explored in TTN’s guide to second-round inflation effects. Friday’s result offers a less heated compensation signal. It does not by itself settle the path of interest rates, establish a market reaction or remove pressure from other household expenses.
