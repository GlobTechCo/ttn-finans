EU Gives Czechia and Spain Two Months to Complete Crypto Tax Reporting Rules

EU regulators gave Czechia and Spain two months to complete national DAC8 crypto-asset reporting rules.

TTN Research DeskNov 20, 2026 · 4:37 AM ET

Crypto · November 20, 2026 · 4:37 AM ET

The European Commission has escalated enforcement proceedings against Czechia and Spain over their incomplete implementation of the European Union's cryptocurrency tax-reporting framework, increasing pressure on national authorities to bring domestic legislation into line with the bloc's requirements. Related context: South Korea moves toward another crypto-tax delay, with a legal change still needed

On November 19, the Commission issued reasoned opinions to both countries under the Directive on Administrative Cooperation, commonly known as DAC8. The two governments now have two months to respond and take the measures necessary to address the identified shortcomings.

The development matters for the European digital-asset industry because DAC8 establishes a system through which tax authorities can exchange information about reportable cryptocurrency transactions. The framework is intended to reduce opportunities for tax evasion and improve transparency across a market in which transactions frequently involve multiple jurisdictions.

The Commission's action does not introduce a new cryptocurrency tax or establish that the two countries have been found guilty by a court. It represents a formal stage in the EU infringement process and gives national authorities another opportunity to complete their implementation obligations.

What the Commission has requested

The Commission said Czechia and Spain had not fully transposed Directive (EU) 2023/2226 into their national legal systems.

The two countries had previously received formal notices in January 2026 concerning incomplete implementation. The November 19 decisions represent an escalation because the Commission concluded that the outstanding issues had not been adequately resolved.

The cases have been registered separately as INFR(2026)0032 for Czechia and INFR(2026)0060 for Spain.

A reasoned opinion is an important procedural step in EU law. It sets out the Commission's position on a member state's failure to comply with its obligations and gives the country an opportunity to address the issue.

If the responses are considered insufficient, the Commission may decide to refer either case to the Court of Justice of the European Union. Financial sanctions could eventually become possible through the judicial process, but they are not automatic consequences of the November decision.

How DAC8 changes cryptocurrency reporting

DAC8 extends the EU's existing system of tax information exchange to cover relevant crypto-assets and service providers. Related context: US Crypto Taxes Begin With the Type of Transaction

Under the framework, reporting crypto-asset service providers must collect information about reportable transactions involving users who are tax residents of EU member states.

The system covers more than conventional cryptocurrency exchanges. Its scope is based on the relevant definitions in the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, or MiCA, and includes a wider range of digital assets.

Stablecoins, certain non-fungible tokens and crypto-assets issued in a decentralised manner are also included where they meet the applicable definitions.

The framework also addresses cross-border activity. A crypto service provider operating in one EU country may serve customers who are tax residents in several other member states.

Reporting obligations started in 2026

The framework applies from January 1, 2026. Reporting service providers must collect relevant user and transaction information from the beginning of the first reporting year.

According to the European Commission's implementation guidance, reporting providers must submit the relevant information to national tax authorities during the following year. The first exchanges of information between EU member states are scheduled to take place by September 30, 2027.

Companies operating across borders need to understand which national authority is responsible for their registration, what information they must collect and how reporting procedures apply to their activities.

Providers that operate in the EU but are not authorised under MiCA may also face single-registration requirements in a member state under the DAC8 framework.

Why the changes matter for crypto exchanges

Businesses operating in several European markets may have to coordinate their compliance procedures across different national authorities. This can increase administrative costs, particularly for smaller providers that do not have the same compliance resources as large international exchanges.

For established exchanges, the changes are likely to require continued investment in compliance systems, data management and regulatory reporting.

DAC8 does not replace individual tax obligations

An important distinction is that reporting information to tax authorities and determining an individual's tax liability are separate matters.

DAC8 establishes mechanisms for collecting and exchanging information. It does not independently determine how much tax a person owes on a cryptocurrency transaction.

For individuals, this means that the introduction of automated reporting should not be confused with a change in the underlying tax rules. Existing obligations to declare taxable income remain relevant.

The increased availability of transaction information may, however, make it easier for authorities to identify cases where declarations do not correspond with reported activity.

What happens next

Czechia and Spain now have two months to respond to the Commission's reasoned opinions and take the necessary implementation measures.

The Commission will assess the responses before deciding whether further action is required.

The next major milestone for the wider DAC8 framework will be the completion of the first reporting cycle covering transactions carried out during 2026.

By September 30, 2027, the first automatic exchanges between participating EU tax authorities are expected to have taken place.

For the cryptocurrency industry, the November enforcement decisions demonstrate that the EU intends to pursue implementation of its reporting framework across all member states.

The immediate cases concern two national governments, but their implications extend beyond Czechia and Spain. The effectiveness of DAC8 depends on consistent implementation across the entire bloc.

As reporting obligations become operational, the ability of crypto service providers to maintain accurate customer records and transaction information will become an increasingly important part of operating in the European market.
