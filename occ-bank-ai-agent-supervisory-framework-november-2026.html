OCC Puts AI Agents’ Financial Powers at the Center of Bank Examinations

The U.S. banking regulator’s new examiner guide focuses on what AI systems can do, the consequences of errors and human oversight. It creates no separate AI risk category or new enforceable standard.

TTN Research Desk2026-11-04

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued a framework on November 4 directing bank examiners to consider the consequences of artificial intelligence use, including how much authority autonomous systems receive. The financial importance lies in the connection between software and decisions: an agent able to initiate a transaction can create a different exposure from a model that only helps an employee prepare an internal document.

The agency’s bulletin says the framework is a resource for its examination workforce. It does not create enforceable standards or new regulatory expectations for banks. Examiners should tailor their work to the institution and the materiality of each use case. Banks are not required to establish a separate AI risk appetite, and the technology is not a standalone risk category.

The official document uses the term “super intelligence,” encompassing technologies previously described as artificial intelligence. That terminology should not be read as a finding that a bank has deployed a system exceeding human capabilities. The practical subject is the use of these technologies in ordinary banking activities, including systems that can take actions rather than simply generate information.

Authority changes the exposure

Reuters, which first reported the guidance, described the distinction between a relatively simple agent authorized to initiate transactions and a sophisticated model used internally. The former can be more consequential despite being technically less advanced. Model size, training cost and a vendor’s performance claims are therefore incomplete ways to judge the financial risk of a deployment.

Consider an illustrative payment workflow. Software that drafts an instruction for a person to check has one set of failure paths. Giving the same software permission to select the beneficiary and submit the payment changes the consequences of an error. The analysis must include the account permissions and the approval process, not just the quality of the generated text.

This is where AI governance meets ordinary access management. A highly accurate system can still be dangerous if it has permissions unrelated to its task. A weaker system with narrow permissions may be easier to contain. Our report on identity controls for AI agents examines why assigning and restricting those permissions has become a distinct operational challenge.

Existing controls remain relevant

The OCC says its guide can supplement existing governance, technology, compliance, operational-risk and model-risk practices. Its stated approach is proportionate: the potential consequences, autonomy and degree of human oversight matter alongside the bank’s size and complexity. That is different from assuming every AI tool deserves the same examination effort simply because it carries the same technology label.

For a bank assessing the economics of an agent, the cost calculation extends beyond the model’s usage bill. Reviews, access controls, testing, monitoring and handling exceptions consume resources. If those costs were excluded from a business case, apparently attractive automation savings could narrow as the system moves from a demonstration into a production workflow.

Our analysis of enterprise AI returns explains why the work left for employees is central to that calculation. A human approval step can reduce the consequences of a mistaken action, but it also retains labor in the process. Removing that step might improve throughput while increasing the importance of other safeguards. Neither choice can be evaluated from a headline model benchmark.

Buying software does not transfer accountability

Third-party relationships supply another established supervisory context. Interagency guidance issued in 2023 describes a risk-management life cycle and calls for practices proportionate to the relationship’s criticality and the bank’s complexity. It recognizes that vendors do not all create the same exposure. Wednesday’s AI framework should be understood alongside that existing treatment of outsourced activities.

For example, a bank could buy an agent from a software company but retain responsibility for choosing its access and deciding when its output becomes an instruction. Vendor documentation might explain how a model behaves without establishing whether the bank’s workflow is adequately controlled. An attractive product specification is therefore only part of the evidence needed to assess a particular deployment.

NIST’s voluntary AI Risk Management Framework provides a separate reference for considering trustworthiness across design, development, use and evaluation. It is not a bank examination rule, and adopting it does not establish OCC approval. Its relevance here is the broader lifecycle: evaluating a system once before launch cannot answer every question about later changes in its use.

The commercial implications

The immediate market implication is a clearer focus for conversations between banks and AI suppliers. Products that can explain their permissions, preserve useful records and support human review may be easier to assess than tools marketed primarily through speed or autonomy. That is an analytical implication of the supervisory approach, not a forecast of revenue for any particular vendor.

The framework also leaves room for low-consequence applications to receive a different level of scrutiny from systems with direct financial authority. It does not announce a general ban on autonomous banking tools or certify any deployment as safe. For banks pursuing productivity gains, the practical test is whether the authority granted to an agent is justified by the task and supported by controls appropriate to what could go wrong.
