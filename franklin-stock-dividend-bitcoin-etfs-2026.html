Franklin Templeton Launches ETFs That Put Stock Dividends Into Bitcoin

Two new Franklin ETFs start with 95% stocks and 5% bitcoin exposure. Their rules redirect dividends into crypto, with limits on how large that slice can grow.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-29

Franklin Templeton launched two exchange-traded funds Thursday that combine U.S. stocks with bitcoin exposure and direct stock dividends toward the crypto allocation. The products package a recurring portfolio decision into a single fund: income generated by the equity holdings helps build exposure to a different, more volatile asset.

The Franklin U.S. Equity Bitcoin DRIP Index ETF, trading under FBEQ, pairs bitcoin with a broad large-company stock index. The Franklin U.S. Innovation Bitcoin DRIP Index ETF, FIBT, uses an index of 100 large Nasdaq-listed U.S. issuers excluding financial companies. Both begin with 95% equity exposure and 5% allocated to bitcoin.

Annual operating expenses are listed at 0.09% for FBEQ and 0.19% for FIBT. The funds’ announced Nasdaq listings and investment strategies are distinct from a forecast of returns. Franklin is offering a method for combining assets, rather than a guarantee that the combination will outperform stocks or bitcoin held separately.

Dividends change destination

An ordinary dividend-reinvestment strategy buys more shares of the asset that generated the payment. Here, the index rules send those payments toward bitcoin instead. The economic trade-off is straightforward: the money used to build crypto exposure is money that is no longer being reinvested into the original equity holdings.

For example, in a simplified $10,000 portfolio starting at the stated proportions, $9,500 would correspond to equities and $500 to bitcoin exposure. If the stock component generated $100 in dividends, redirecting that amount would add to the crypto side rather than purchasing additional stock exposure. This is an illustration of the allocation mechanism, excluding price changes, costs and the funds’ detailed trading implementation.

The approach therefore differs from adding fresh cash to bitcoin every month. Its contribution depends on the dividends generated by the stock holdings. A portfolio of companies paying less income supplies less through that channel, even when their share prices rise strongly. The choice between the two equity indexes is consequently meaningful, not just a different label on the same crypto allocation.

The bitcoin slice is allowed to move, within rules

The prospectus describes quarterly checks on the index allocation. If bitcoin exposure exceeds 5% at a quarterly rebalance, it is reduced to 4.5%. If it is 5% or less, it remains unchanged. A separate rule applies if the allocation exceeds 20% between quarterly rebalances, bringing it back to 4.5% on the close of the second following business day.

That means 5% is not a continuously maintained target or a guarantee that the crypto weight stays unchanged throughout the quarter. Price movements and reinvested dividends can alter it. The rules also mean a falling bitcoin allocation is not automatically replenished to 5% at every scheduled review.

The funds seek bitcoin exposure through instruments such as bitcoin exchange-traded products, derivatives and depositary receipts, with a subsidiary permitted for certain investments. Investors should distinguish a portfolio allocation described as bitcoin exposure from direct ownership of coins in a personal wallet. One ticker does not remove the underlying risks

The convenience is administrative: an investor can hold one fund while its methodology manages the connection between the stock portfolio and crypto allocation. It does not make stock dividends an extra source of free money or protect the initial principal. Dividends are one component of equity returns, and their use changes the portfolio’s future exposures.

At the stated annual expense rates, a constant $10,000 holding implies approximately $9 or $19 in operating expenses over a year, before other costs and market movements. That simple comparison does not capture brokerage charges, trading spreads or how effectively the portfolio follows its index. The mechanics linking ETF shares to their holdings remain relevant to the price an investor actually pays.

The larger uncertainty is investment performance. Stocks and bitcoin can both fall, and an allocation spanning the two does not ensure that one will offset losses in the other. As explained in TTN’s discussion of changing correlations, diversification depends on how assets behave together, especially during stress.

The launch extends competition in crypto investing beyond funds that simply track bitcoin. Its distinctive feature is the rule connecting stock income with the crypto allocation. Evaluating it requires understanding that rule, the chosen equity exposure and the rebalancing limits, rather than treating the word dividend as a promise of dependable income or lower risk.
