U.S. Jobless Claims Fall to 194,000 as Continuing Claims Rise

New unemployment claims fell by 6,000, while continuing claims increased. The two measures give different readings on the labor market.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-22

New U.S. unemployment benefit claims fell to 194,000 in the week ended October 17, the Labor Department reported Thursday, suggesting employers remain reluctant to shed workers. Continuing claims moved in the opposite direction, however, offering a reminder that a low rate of new layoffs does not necessarily mean finding another job is easy.

Initial claims declined by 6,000 from the previous week’s revised 200,000. Economists polled by Reuters had expected 200,000. The report therefore delivered a firmer reading than that forecast, but its strongest message concerns job retention rather than the pace at which businesses are recruiting. Those are separate parts of the employment cycle.

Fewer new filings, more people remaining on benefits

The number receiving ongoing state unemployment benefits rose by 25,000 to a seasonally adjusted 1.728 million in the week ended October 10. That measure is reported with a one-week lag relative to initial claims. The two figures should not be treated as observations of precisely the same seven-day period.

The four-week average of new claims fell by 1,000 to 197,750. The corresponding average for continuing claims increased by 4,000 to 1.711 million. Looking at both averages reduces the influence of a single week and makes the divergence clearer: new applications eased, while the ongoing beneficiary count edged higher.

Our guide to weekly jobless claims explains why the distinction matters. Initial filings help track the flow of workers entering the benefit system. Continuing claims describe people still receiving benefits. Neither is a complete measure of everyone out of work, and eligibility rules mean some unemployed workers never appear in these totals.

Revisions change the comparison

The prior week’s initial reading was revised upward by 1,000, from 199,000 to 200,000. That makes the latest decline 6,000, rather than the 5,000 a reader might calculate using the previously published number. The previous continuing-claims level was revised down by 7,000 to 1.703 million, creating the correct base for this week’s increase.

Seasonal adjustment also matters. Actual, unadjusted new applications totaled 172,390, down 19,321. The department’s seasonal factors had anticipated a decline of 14,179. Adjustment accounts for recurring calendar patterns; it does not create a separate group of claimants. Readers should compare adjusted figures with adjusted figures and avoid mixing the two series to calculate a trend.

The insured unemployment rate remained at 1.1% for the week ended October 10. This rate covers the population insured under the relevant benefit programs. It is different from the broader unemployment rate produced by the monthly household survey, which our unemployment and participation explainer examines in detail.

A layoff signal cannot substitute for a hiring measure

For households, fewer new benefit applications are reassuring because they point away from a broad acceleration in job losses. But someone already searching for work faces a different question: how many suitable openings are available and how quickly employers are filling them. A rising continuing count can reflect difficulty leaving benefits, although it does not establish a single cause.

Businesses and investors therefore need the next monthly employment report and hiring data to complete the picture. Payroll growth, labor-force participation and wage measures capture developments that weekly benefit filings cannot. Our guide to the payrolls report sets out why a change in employment should be assessed alongside revisions and the household survey.

The monetary-policy implications are similarly conditional. A labor market that avoids heavy layoffs can support consumer spending, but this release does not measure inflation or determine the Federal Reserve’s next decision. Reading an automatic interest-rate response into one weekly number would give the report more forecasting power than it has.

Thursday’s release leaves a specific combination to watch: relatively few new claimants alongside a modest increase in continuing beneficiaries. If that pattern persists, attention will remain on the pace of hiring and movement out of unemployment. The next weekly release will also show whether today’s estimates survive revision.
