Asian Stocks Drop 3.2% as Tariff Fears Hit Tech Shares

Tariff uncertainty, a technology retreat and hotter Australian inflation unsettled Asia-Pacific markets and complicated the global rate outlook.

TTN Research DeskNov 25, 2026 · 3:25 AM EST

Asian equities fell on November 25 as renewed US tariff threats met a retreat in technology shares and hotter Australian inflation. MSCI’s broadest Asia-Pacific index excluding Japan dropped 3.2%, while its global share gauge fell 0.4%, Reuters reported. Europe moved in the opposite direction: regional shares rose 0.5% and auto stocks gained 2.4%. The split points to a concentrated repricing in export- and technology-heavy Asian markets, rather than a uniform selloff across global equities.

The immediate trade concern was a US investigation into alleged excess industrial capacity among 16 major trading partners. The Trump administration was expected to release findings that could lead to new import duties, Reuters reported, citing three people familiar with the matter. The findings had not been made public at the time of the report. Investors were weighing the possibility of new tariffs, not reacting to a confirmed list of products, rates or start dates. The eventual impact will depend on the measures announced, how tariff costs are distributed and any response from trading partners.

Uncertainty can affect business decisions before duties take effect. Manufacturers may bring shipments forward, delay investment or reconsider where components are assembled. Asian supply chains cross several borders: a product assembled in one economy may rely on semiconductors, displays and machinery sourced elsewhere. A tariff aimed at one country can therefore raise costs for suppliers that do not sell the finished product directly into the United States. Companies with complex logistics have less room to absorb disruption.

Technology shares magnify the regional move

The tariff risk arrived as investors were already cutting exposure to technology. S&P 500 e-mini futures were down 0.8% and Nasdaq futures fell 1.9%, Reuters reported. On November 24, the S&P 500 lost 0.5% and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.5%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 0.9%. Nvidia shares dropped 5.9% and Anthropic fell 6.5%, moving below its IPO price. The previous session’s weakness was concentrated in technology rather than spread evenly across the US market.

Taiwan and South Korea are particularly exposed to shifts in semiconductor demand because technology companies carry significant weight in their equity markets. Investors are also testing whether expected returns from AI infrastructure can justify the scale of spending. Higher financing costs, export restrictions and uncertainty about customer budgets can affect data-centre economics. The move does not prove that AI demand has collapsed; it shows that valuations remain sensitive to growth, funding and cash returns. Australian inflation complicates the rate outlook

Australia added a separate macroeconomic concern. The Australian Bureau of Statistics said consumer prices rose 4.9% in the year to October, up from 4.3% in September. Trimmed-mean inflation accelerated to 3.8% from 3.6%, while the CPI increased 0.7% in seasonally adjusted monthly terms. Annual inflation in transport reached 10.6% and housing costs rose 6.4%, according to the ABS. Both headline and underlying measures were above the Reserve Bank of Australia’s 2%–3% target range.

Persistent inflation limits the scope for near-term rate relief, but it does not dictate a single policy response. The RBA must weigh price pressures against the effects of restrictive borrowing costs on households, employment and business investment, a trade-off examined in TTN’s guide to central-bank policy divergence. Reuters reported that the central bank had raised rates by a cumulative 125 basis points during 2026. The latest data make the timing of any future easing less certain, rather than proving another increase is inevitable. Bond demand adds another signal

Japan’s November 25 auction of 40-year government bonds received 786.5 billion yen in competitive bids and accepted 299.6 billion yen, according to the Ministry of Finance. Dividing bids by the amount accepted gives a bid-to-cover ratio of about 2.63, down from approximately 3.10 at the September auction. The highest accepted yield was 4.090%, compared with 4.125% at the earlier sale. The ratio points to weaker demand at this auction, but the lower accepted yield means not every measure of borrowing cost rose. The US 10-year Treasury yield was around 4.898% in Reuters’ snapshot.

Long-term yields influence the discount rates used to value future earnings and financing costs for governments and capital-intensive companies; TTN’s bond-duration explainer covers the price sensitivity. The Japanese auction is one data point, not evidence of a global bond-market break. Investors were also awaiting US personal income and outlays data, including the Federal Reserve’s preferred core personal consumption expenditures inflation measure, due later on November 25. That release could shift expectations for US monetary policy, while tariffs could affect future costs through imports and supply chains.

The risks are connected but distinct. Tariffs could disrupt trade and raise costs; persistent inflation could keep financing conditions restrictive; and a reassessment of AI valuations could weigh on technology-heavy markets. For companies, the combination may delay investment and pressure margins. For investors, the next evidence will be the US trade findings, any resulting policy measures and the inflation data. Until those details emerge, the fall in Asian shares is best read as a repricing of trade and technology risk, not confirmation that global demand or AI infrastructure spending has entered a sustained downturn.
