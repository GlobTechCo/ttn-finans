US Goods Trade Deficit Widens as Capital-Goods Imports Surge

September imports rose faster than exports. The figures complicate the growth picture ahead of the third-quarter GDP report.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-28

The U.S. goods trade deficit widened to $134.9 billion in September as imports of capital equipment surged, adding a complication to the growth picture ahead of Thursday's third-quarter gross domestic product report. Census Bureau figures released Wednesday showed imports rising faster than exports even though overseas sales also increased. The shortfall grew $2.8 billion from August, or 2.1%.

Imports reached $344.1 billion, an increase of $8.1 billion, while exports rose $5.3 billion to $209.2 billion. The difference matters for interpreting the report: the widening gap did not result from an overall collapse in American exports. Demand for foreign goods simply expanded by more in dollar terms than shipments going the other way.

Reuters linked the import strength to the buildout of artificial-intelligence infrastructure. The official end-use tables show a sharp increase in capital goods, a broad equipment category rather than an exclusive measure of AI purchases. Treating every dollar in that category as a data-center investment would overstate what the government release establishes.

Equipment explains much of the increase

Seasonally adjusted capital-goods imports climbed 7.5% from August to about $157.4 billion. They were 77.2% above September 2025. The monthly increase in that category, roughly $10.9 billion using the published tables, exceeded the rise in total goods imports because declines elsewhere offset part of the gain. Industrial-supply imports fell 6%, while vehicle imports dropped 4%.

Consumer-goods imports increased 3.1% during the month, although they remained below their year-earlier level. That mixed comparison cautions against describing every part of import demand as uniformly strong. Food imports also declined. On the export side, capital goods rose 5.4%, food shipments increased 12.8%, and motor vehicles fell 5.6%, illustrating different conditions across industries.

For companies building computing capacity, imported equipment is an input into an investment program. The commercial question comes later: whether the completed capacity produces sufficient revenue and profit. TTN's analysis of AI investment and monetization explains why purchasing infrastructure and earning an adequate return on it are separate milestones.

A larger deficit is not a standalone recession signal

GDP measures production inside the United States. Spending on imported equipment can appear within investment, but the foreign-produced portion must be removed through the imports adjustment. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis explains that this subtraction prevents overseas production from being counted as American output; buying an import does not itself destroy an equal amount of domestic production.

That distinction allows vigorous investment spending and a negative contribution from net exports to appear in the same growth report. Wednesday's dollar-denominated goods balance cannot, by itself, establish the size of the contribution to inflation-adjusted quarterly GDP. The calculation also needs price adjustments, the full quarter, services trade and other national-accounting information.

The advance trade estimates are seasonally adjusted but not adjusted for price changes. A larger dollar total therefore is not automatically an equivalent increase in physical shipments. The report also uses Census definitions for goods, rather than presenting the complete goods-and-services balance. Our guide to tariffs and import costs examines another distinction relevant to businesses: border measures, merchandise values and the final costs customers face are different quantities.

Inventories add another piece of the growth puzzle

The same release put wholesale inventories at $1,011.5 billion, up 0.6% from August. Retail inventories increased 0.3% to $885.3 billion. These are end-of-month stocks, not sales figures. An increase can accompany planned restocking, supply arriving ahead of demand or slower movement of merchandise; the inventory level alone cannot identify which explanation dominates.

Thursday's GDP release will put those flows alongside consumption, investment and government activity. For readers assessing the economy, the useful comparison is between domestic demand and domestic production, rather than a single trade number labeled good or bad. September's report shows a larger import bill and expanding exports, with equipment demand central to the difference. It leaves the final third-quarter growth calculation to the broader accounts.
