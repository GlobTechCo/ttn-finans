British retail sales fall 0.4%, but the quarter still ends higher

Weak autumn clothing demand pulled September sales lower. Upward revisions and a stronger quarter complicate the picture for consumers and rates.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-23

British shoppers bought fewer goods in September, with unusually warm weather weakening demand for autumn clothing and supermarket sales slipping. Retail sales volumes fell 0.4% from August, the Office for National Statistics said October 23, although upward revisions to earlier months left the third quarter stronger than the latest monthly figure might suggest.

The quantity sold increased 0.7% in July through September compared with the previous quarter. September sales were also 0.8% above a year earlier. Those comparisons matter: a monthly retreat can signal a change in momentum without establishing that spending has contracted over a longer period. The release covers Great Britain, rather than every part of the United Kingdom.

The revisions change the starting point

The ONS bulletin revised August’s increase to 0.7% from 0.5% and July’s decline to 0.4% from 0.5%. September therefore followed a stronger August than initially reported. Comparing the new monthly reading with an unrevised prior estimate would miss part of the change in the underlying series.

Online clothing retailers reported that autumn and winter ranges sold poorly in the warm conditions. Supermarkets also lost ground after gains in July and August, while non-food store volumes fell 0.4%. Automotive fuel moved in the opposite direction. The weakness was consequently uneven across products, rather than a uniform retreat by every type of retailer.

For clothing sellers, the commercial issue extends beyond a delayed purchase. If demand arrives after the intended selling season, retailers may need discounts to clear stock. That can support the number of items sold while reducing profit per item. This is an implication of the reported pattern, not evidence that every affected chain has already cut its earnings forecast.

Cash spent and goods purchased can diverge

Retail volumes measure quantities after allowing for price changes. They answer a different question from a retailer’s cash receipts. A household can spend more money and leave with fewer goods if prices rise sufficiently. Our guide to inflation and retail spending explains why a nominal sales increase should not automatically be treated as stronger purchasing power.

Britain’s separate inflation release this week put September consumer price inflation at 3.5%, up from 3.1% in August. Motor fuels were the largest upward contributor to the change in the annual rate. That is relevant context for household budgets, but an annual inflation reading and a monthly retail volume change cannot be subtracted from each other to produce a meaningful measure of real spending growth.

The distinction also matters for company results. A retailer’s revenue can reflect new stores, changes in product mix, price increases and overseas operations, as well as the number of goods sold. The national release is therefore a demand indicator, not a direct forecast for a particular chain’s profit. Our retailer earnings explainer sets out those differences.

A different survey gives a stronger signal

Friday’s business survey offered a counterweight to the retail figures. Reuters reported that the preliminary S&P Global UK composite purchasing managers’ index rose to 53.1 in October from 52.0 in September, its highest reading since February. That survey covers a broader mix of private-sector activity and a later month than the retail release.

There is no requirement for those indicators to move together. Services and business investment can improve while households buy fewer discretionary goods. A purchasing managers’ index measures the breadth of reported changes among surveyed firms; its numerical level is not a percentage growth rate and is not directly comparable with the ONS sales estimate.

For GBP/USD and interest-rate expectations, the combined message is mixed: weaker goods demand sits alongside persistent inflation and stronger reported business activity. None of those releases alone determines the Bank of England’s next decision. The next useful evidence will be whether the clothing slowdown reverses, whether broader household spending holds up, and whether price pressures ease without a deeper loss of demand.
