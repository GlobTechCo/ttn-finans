Bitcoin Falls to a Two-Year Low as AI Sell-Off Spreads Across Risk Assets

Bitcoin's two-year low shows how quickly AI-driven stock turmoil can spill into crypto, even without a crypto-specific shock.

TTN Research DeskNov 21, 2026 · 5:57 AM EST

Bitcoin slid to its lowest level in two years on Friday as a technology-led sell-off on global markets intensified pressure on riskier assets. The largest cryptocurrency fell 5.10% to $55,830 by 11:13 p.m. Eastern Time, according to Reuters, marking its weakest level since September 2024. It had earlier dropped below $60,000 for the first time since July.

The decline extended a difficult period for digital assets. Ether, the second-largest cryptocurrency, was down 5.23% at $1,648.91 in Reuters’ earlier Friday market report, while Bitcoin was then quoted at $59,381. The later move to $55,830 showed that selling accelerated as the US trading session progressed. A separate Reuters update placed Bitcoin at $56,813.68 at 10:50 p.m. ET, illustrating how quickly prices were moving.

The immediate catalyst was not a new failure in Bitcoin’s network or a single cryptocurrency company. Instead, digital assets were caught in a wider retreat from risk. Investors were reducing exposure to technology shares after doubts emerged about the valuations of AI-related businesses and the returns expected from their enormous infrastructure spending. When risk appetite weakens across markets, assets viewed as volatile or dependent on speculative demand can face selling even without a crypto-specific trigger.

That connection was visible in the scale of the equity decline. US chipmakers fell sharply, the Nasdaq dropped more than 5% on Friday, and the S&P 500 entered correction territory after moving more than 10% below its October record. Asian technology markets also suffered heavy losses. Reuters reported that concerns about the profitability of AI investment, higher borrowing costs and the possibility of further Federal Reserve tightening had contributed to the broader market rout.

For Bitcoin, the macroeconomic channel matters because its valuation does not rest on a stream of corporate earnings or contractual cash flows. Investors often assess it through scarcity, network adoption, liquidity, regulation and expectations about future demand. Those factors can support long-term investment arguments, but they do not insulate the price from changes in financing conditions. When cash and short-term government securities offer more attractive yields, the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding, volatile asset can rise.

Interest-rate expectations are particularly important for leveraged traders. Higher financing costs can make borrowed positions more expensive to maintain, while falling prices can push collateral values toward liquidation thresholds. If exchanges automatically close positions when margin requirements are breached, forced selling can add to an initial decline. Public price data alone does not establish how much of Friday's move came from liquidations, however, and it would be misleading to assign a precise share without verified derivatives-market figures.

The sell-off also raises questions about the relationship between Bitcoin and institutional investment products. Spot exchange-traded funds have created a familiar route for some investors to gain exposure without directly holding tokens. But ETF access does not eliminate market risk. Fund shares reflect the underlying asset, and institutional flows can change with portfolio risk limits, redemptions and broader allocations. The Reuters reports cited here identify macroeconomic and AI-related concerns as pressures; they do not establish a specific ETF outflow figure as the cause of the latest low.

Crypto markets have also faced other sources of uncertainty. Reuters has cited rising borrowing costs and regulatory risks following the US midterm elections among concerns weighing on digital assets. Those issues can influence expectations about exchange operations, token distribution, stablecoin rules and the willingness of traditional financial institutions to expand crypto services. They are separate from the technical operation of Bitcoin itself, but they affect the environment in which investors buy, sell and custody the asset. TTN's coverage of the EU's crypto tax-reporting deadlines shows how regulatory requirements are evolving across markets.

The distinction between the Bitcoin network and the market price is essential. A lower exchange price does not mean that the blockchain has stopped processing transactions or that its supply rules have changed. It means buyers are currently willing to pay less for exposure. Network security, transaction activity, liquidity on trading venues and the financial condition of intermediaries require separate analysis. Conflating those measures can turn a market decline into an unsupported claim about the technology.

For crypto businesses, a prolonged downturn can have direct commercial consequences. Exchanges may see lower trading volumes if customers withdraw from the market, although volatility can temporarily increase transaction activity. Mining companies face pressure when the value of their Bitcoin output falls while electricity, equipment and financing costs remain. Firms holding Bitcoin on their balance sheets may also face a decline in the market value of those assets, potentially affecting investor confidence and access to capital. Custody risks are illustrated by an Abu Dhabi court order freezing assets linked to a Binance heist.

The immediate question is whether Bitcoin can stabilize as global markets find a floor or whether further deleveraging will extend the decline. A rebound in technology shares and improved expectations for interest rates could support risk appetite, but neither outcome is assured. Conversely, a sustained price decline would not by itself prove that Bitcoin's long-term adoption thesis has failed. It would demonstrate that the asset remains exposed to liquidity cycles and investor positioning.

For now, the two-year low is a warning about correlation and risk management. Bitcoin is traded as a distinct digital asset, but in periods of market stress it can behave like a high-volatility risk investment. Investors should distinguish the network's underlying characteristics from the price dynamics of leveraged, globally traded markets.
