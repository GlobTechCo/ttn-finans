Global Banks’ $301 Billion Trading Boom Faces a Tougher Test in 2027

Global markets revenue is forecast to hit a record, but uneven trading growth raises questions about bank earnings durability.

TTN Research DeskNov 24, 2026 · 8:13 AM EST

Global banks’ markets businesses are heading for their strongest year on record, but the source of the boom is uneven and its durability is already in question. Crisil Coalition Greenwich estimates that revenue across more than 250 banks will reach $301.1 billion in 2026, 14% above 2025 and higher than the previous peak of $286.9 billion in 2009. The figure captures a powerful year for trading desks. It does not, by itself, show that banks have become structurally more profitable or that the same income can be repeated next year.

The estimate, reported by Financial News on November 24, puts revenue for the first three quarters at $233.8 billion, up 16% from the same period a year earlier. The full-year forecast is built around two very different markets businesses. Equities revenue is expected to rise 32% to $128.7 billion, while fixed income, currencies and commodities, or FICC, is projected to increase just 3.3% to $172.4 billion. Together, those two figures account for the $301.1 billion total.

One distinction matters when reading the breakdown: prime services is not a third revenue pool to add to equities and FICC. It is a component of the equities franchise. Prime services revenue is forecast to climb 41% to $50 billion as hedge funds use more financing, securities lending, clearing and related services. The figure helps explain the strength of equities, but adding it to the $128.7 billion equities estimate would double-count part of the business.

The equity surge reflects a market in which investors have repeatedly had to reassess the value of technology companies and the capital spending behind artificial intelligence. Changes in expectations for chip demand, data-centre construction and the commercial returns from AI have produced sharp rallies and reversals. Those moves create work for cash-equity desks, derivatives teams and prime brokers as clients rebalance portfolios, hedge concentrated positions and adjust leverage. Trading banks can earn from client activity without needing to predict the direction of the market correctly.

That distinction between activity and direction is important. A market-maker earns a spread for providing liquidity and managing inventory; a derivatives desk structures transactions to meet a client’s exposure needs; a prime broker provides financing and operational services to investment funds. Volatile markets can increase demand for all three. But a bank’s eventual return depends on pricing, hedging costs, balance-sheet usage and whether positions are managed without material losses. More trades do not translate automatically into a proportional increase in profit.

The prime-services forecast is particularly notable because it points to activity beyond conventional share dealing. Hedge funds can generate financing and securities-lending revenue for banks when they build long and short positions, borrow stock or use leverage. The 41% projected increase suggests that institutions are not merely trading more frequently; at least some are using a wider set of services to implement strategies. That can deepen client relationships, although it also exposes banks to counterparty and collateral risks if market stress forces funds to reduce positions quickly.

FICC remains the larger of the two broad revenue categories, but its modest projected growth masks substantial differences between products. G10 rates revenue is expected to fall 9.5% to $36 billion, even as the wider FICC pool rises. That divergence matters because rates desks are sensitive to changes in central-bank expectations, yield curves and client positioning. A period of uncertainty may prompt more hedging, but it can also leave banks on the wrong side of crowded trades or reduce opportunities when policy expectations become clearer.

Geopolitical disruption has also supported client activity. Financial News linked the 2026 environment to the war involving Iran and wider political uncertainty, which can prompt companies and investors to hedge currency, commodity and interest-rate exposures. Such demand is real business for dealers, but it is not a dependable earnings engine. If tensions ease, commodity markets normalise or monetary policy becomes easier to anticipate, some of the urgency behind those transactions can disappear. A forecast driven by exceptional events should therefore be treated differently from growth generated by recurring client demand.

The industry-wide record also says little about which banks captured the most value. The estimate aggregates a broad group of institutions, while individual results depend on franchise strength, product mix and market share. Large universal banks can combine trading with lending, financing and corporate relationships; specialist electronic firms compete aggressively on execution speed and pricing. Jane Street and Citadel Securities are important competitors in parts of the market, particularly where technology and liquidity provision matter. Traditional banks retain balance-sheet capacity and broad institutional relationships, but those advantages do not guarantee that revenue growth will be evenly distributed.

There is a further gap between markets revenue and shareholder returns. Trading businesses consume capital, technology budgets and compensation, and their risk profiles can change quickly. A record revenue year may support earnings and returns on tangible equity, but investors need to examine the cost base, credit exposures, risk-weighted assets and the contribution of less volatile businesses. Revenue is an input into profitability, not a substitute for it. Banks that turn higher volumes into durable client franchises will be better placed than those that simply benefit from a temporary burst of volatility.

The outlook for 2027 is consequently more cautious. Coalition Greenwich expects activity to moderate, with FICC revenue potentially declining marginally as commodity conditions normalise. Financial News also reported that major banks had already seen slower quarter-on-quarter growth in markets revenue through September, although executives were not signalling an abrupt collapse. Those observations do not prove that the cycle has turned, but they make it harder to extrapolate 2026’s growth rate into the next financial year.

For investors, the central question is not whether $301.1 billion is a record. It is how much of that revenue can survive a less volatile market and how much converts into sustainable returns. The 2026 performance demonstrates the value of scale, market-making infrastructure and institutional client relationships during periods of disruption. It also shows the limits of treating trading windfalls as a permanent earnings base. The banks best positioned for 2027 will be those that preserve client activity, control risk and defend margins when market conditions become less favourable.

Geopolitical shocks have added another source of business. The war involving Iran and wider international uncertainty have encouraged companies, asset managers and financial institutions to hedge currency, commodity and interest-rate exposure. Such activity can increase transaction volumes, but it is not a stable source of earnings. A sudden reduction in tensions or a clearer monetary-policy outlook could reduce the need for frequent portfolio adjustments.

The revenue forecast should also be distinguished from the profitability of individual banks. Markets revenue is not the same as net income, and a record for the industry does not mean every institution has benefited equally. Trading businesses require capital, technology, risk management and highly paid specialist staff. Higher activity can increase revenue while also raising operating costs and the potential for losses when positions move against a bank.

Competition is another pressure. Electronic trading firms such as Jane Street and Citadel Securities have expanded their presence in financial markets, challenging traditional banks in areas where speed, pricing and technology are critical. Banks retain advantages in financing, balance-sheet capacity and broad client relationships, but they cannot assume that higher volumes will automatically translate into greater market share.

The outlook for 2027 is therefore more complicated than the record forecast for this year suggests. Coalition Greenwich expects a moderation in activity, with global FICC revenue likely to decline marginally as commodity-market conditions normalise. Equities trading may also face a tougher comparison if AI-related volatility becomes less intense. The largest banks have already reported slower quarter-on-quarter growth in markets revenue through September, although executives have played down concerns about a sharp reversal.

For investors, the key issue is the quality and durability of trading income. A strong year can support bank earnings, improve returns on capital and provide a useful offset when lending or investment-banking activity is weaker. But markets businesses remain sensitive to volatility, client positioning and risk appetite. If the record is driven partly by exceptional events, it should not automatically be treated as a new baseline for valuation.

The $301.1 billion forecast demonstrates how valuable market-making and institutional trading can become during periods of uncertainty. It also illustrates why the same conditions that lift bank revenue can make future performance harder to predict. The record may be within reach in 2026, but sustaining it will depend on whether banks can maintain client activity after the current wave of market disruption passes.
