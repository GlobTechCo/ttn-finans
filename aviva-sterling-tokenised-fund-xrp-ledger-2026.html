Aviva Investors Launches Tokenized Sterling Fund Class on XRP Ledger

Aviva seeded the new fund share class with £50 million; the launch adds blockchain access to an existing sterling money-market portfolio.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-26

Aviva Investors has launched a tokenized share class of its Sterling Liquidity Fund on the XRP Ledger, adding blockchain access to an existing money-market portfolio. Aviva invested £50 million in the new class, according to Monday's joint announcement with Ripple. The development concerns how investors can hold a fund interest, rather than a new cryptocurrency investment strategy.

The size distinction matters. Approximately £22 billion refers to the whole underlying fund, not the amount newly tokenized. The announcement says more than £86 million is invested across Aviva Investors' two tokenized share classes. Those figures should not be combined or presented as evidence that the entire conventional portfolio has moved onto a blockchain.

The new class uses a digital-twin structure: holdings can be represented on-chain while the established off-chain fund remains in place. It is available to eligible investors with digital wallets. Our coverage of tokenized securities and investor rights explains why the legal relationship behind a token matters as much as the network carrying it.

A fund interest is different from XRP

XRP is the network's native asset; a tokenized fund holding represents a different economic claim. The XRP Ledger's documentation explains that issued tokens can represent assets outside the blockchain. Recording a fund interest there does not turn the underlying portfolio into XRP or give XRP holders a claim on the fund's assets.

The network also charges transaction costs in XRP. That operational use should not be confused with buying XRP equal to the value of the fund. The launch announcement does not establish that Aviva purchased £50 million of the native token, and it provides no basis for translating the fund's assets into an equivalent amount of new XRP investment demand.

For readers following institutional adoption, that separation makes the news more useful. A blockchain can become part of a financial institution's operating process without the institution taking the same exposure as someone buying the network's traded token. Adoption, transaction activity and token-price performance are related questions, but they are not identical measures.

The underlying investment still has risks

Aviva's conventional Sterling Liquidity Fund page describes a portfolio seeking returns in line with money-market rates while aiming to preserve investment value. It normally holds short-term deposits, reverse repurchase agreements and cash-like instruments issued by governments, banks and companies. The objective provides context for the fund; it is not a guarantee that an investor cannot lose money.

The same page warns that the value of money-market securities can change with interest rates, inflation and issuer creditworthiness. It also distinguishes the fund from a bank deposit and says principal can be lost. These underlying investment risks do not disappear because ownership records are available through a digital access layer.

Our comparison of money-market funds and savings accounts explains those differences. The product may serve a cash-management purpose, but the rights and protections of a fund investor differ from those of a depositor. A new distribution channel does not erase that distinction or automatically broaden who is eligible to invest.

Settlement technology does not replace fund rules

A ledger can record a transfer at a different speed from the processes needed to subscribe, redeem or move the underlying cash. That is why around-the-clock blockchain availability should not be assumed to mean unrestricted, immediate redemption of every fund holding. Investors still need the applicable share-class terms, eligibility requirements and operating procedures.

The conventional class page reviewed here is background on the underlying portfolio. Its displayed fees and minimum subscription belong to that class and should not automatically be assigned to the newly tokenized offering. Comparing the two requires the relevant documents for each, rather than assuming that a common fund name makes every condition identical.

The practical evidence to watch is whether the digital class makes subscription, recordkeeping and transfers more efficient for eligible investors. Growth in actual assets held through the class would be more informative than applying the whole fund's £22 billion size to a blockchain-adoption headline. Monday's launch expands an established financial product's distribution; its commercial impact will depend on how investors use that access.
