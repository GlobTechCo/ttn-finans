SBI and Shinhan Card Plan Yen Stablecoin Payment Tests Between Japan and Korea

The partners will examine retail payments and trade settlement under a preliminary agreement, without announcing a commercial launch date.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-21

Japan’s SBI VC Trade and SBI XDC Network APAC have reached a preliminary agreement with South Korea’s Shinhan Card to examine blockchain-based payments, including the use of yen-denominated stablecoins in physical shops and cross-border settlement. SBI disclosed the agreement Wednesday, presenting it as a framework for testing commercial possibilities rather than an available payment service.

The proposal brings together a digital-asset trading business, a blockchain infrastructure venture and an established card company. Its significance lies in the attempt to connect tokens with existing merchant payment systems. Demonstrating that a token can move between wallets is a different task from making it useful to a traveler buying something at a checkout.

A preliminary agreement with several possible uses

SBI says the companies will explore ways to link digital assets with traditional financial and payment infrastructure. The work includes assessing the user experience, transaction process and technical and operational arrangements for yen-denominated stablecoins at physical merchants. The partners also intend to examine international settlement, including payments for cross-border trade.

Korean reporting places the signing in Tokyo on October 20; the announcement followed on October 21. SBI’s release says concrete initiatives will be disclosed later. It does not announce a commercial launch date, merchant coverage, customer fees or a completed rollout. Those omissions leave the scale and economics of any future service unresolved.

The agreement also includes cooperation around XDC Network infrastructure. That does not mean every possible payment route has been finalized, or that the announcement establishes new demand for a particular tradable token. A partnership concerning a blockchain and a commercial commitment to purchase its native asset are separate propositions.

The checkout still needs a complete settlement process

A yen stablecoin aims to maintain a value linked to Japan’s currency. A Korean merchant, however, may want proceeds in won. As an analytical matter, connecting those two preferences requires decisions about conversion, liquidity and who bears exchange-rate movements. Calling a payment blockchain-based does not itself answer those questions.

The customer’s experience depends on more than the transfer speed recorded on a ledger. Funding a wallet, passing identity checks, paying conversion charges and resolving a failed purchase can all influence whether the service is practical. The announcement does not provide enough detail to compare its full cost with a conventional card purchase or international bank payment.

Trade settlement raises another set of operating questions. A payment can arrive quickly while the goods, invoice or compliance review remains unresolved. A useful trial would therefore need to show how the token transfer fits the broader transaction, including recordkeeping and responsibility when one stage fails. These are evaluation criteria, not features the partners have already demonstrated.

Redemption and controls matter as much as the peg

The Bank for International Settlements has argued that stablecoins must be evaluated against monetary and payment-system requirements, including acceptance at face value and protection against financial crime. Its analysis also distinguishes moving a token from maintaining confidence in the institution and assets behind the token’s promised value.

For a traveler, the practical question is whether the balance can be converted back into usable money on predictable terms. A nominal yen reference does not settle eligibility, redemption timing or the treatment of a service interruption. Our guide to stablecoin backing and redemption risk examines why the promise of a stable value depends on the arrangement supporting it.

For merchants, refunds and reconciliation are equally relevant. A shop needs to match what a customer paid with what it received, handle returns and maintain accounting records. A token-based service can retain familiar card interfaces while changing how funds move behind them, but the operating responsibilities must still be assigned.

The next meaningful milestones will be a defined proof of concept, named settlement arrangements and evidence that ordinary users and merchants can complete transactions reliably. Until those details emerge, the agreement is evidence of institutional interest in stablecoin payments across the Japan-Korea corridor, rather than proof that a cheaper or commercially available alternative has arrived.
