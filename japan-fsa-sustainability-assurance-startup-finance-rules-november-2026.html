Japan FSA Drafts Sustainability Assurance Rules and Eases Startup Fundraising

Japan’s proposed rules would require assurance for major Prime Market issuers and raise the securities-registration exemption threshold for startup offerings to ¥500 million.

TTN Research DeskNov 14, 2026

Japan’s Financial Services Agency has published draft rules that would expand the use of third-party assurance for corporate sustainability disclosures while making it easier for startups to raise capital through certain securities offerings. The proposals form part of Japan’s 2026 amendments to its Financial Instruments and Exchange Act and are scheduled to take effect on April 1, 2027, subject to the completion of the rulemaking process.

The package, released on November 13, addresses two areas that have become increasingly important to Japan’s capital markets: the reliability of non-financial corporate information and access to funding for younger companies. The proposals are intended to establish more detailed rules before the relevant provisions of the amended law enter into force.

Sustainability assurance for large issuers

For sustainability reporting, the draft identifies companies listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange’s Prime Market with an average market capitalization of at least ¥500 billion as the companies required to prepare specified non-financial information under the applicable standards and obtain assurance from an eligible external provider.

The threshold is significant because it defines the initial group of issuers subject to the new requirements. Rather than applying the assurance obligation to every listed company, the FSA has proposed a phased approach based on company size. This is intended to concentrate the initial compliance burden on larger issuers, whose disclosures are more likely to be used by institutional investors and other capital-market participants.

The proposed framework distinguishes between the obligation to prepare sustainability information and the obligation to obtain assurance over that information. The two requirements are subject to different transitional arrangements. This distinction matters because companies may need to begin preparing disclosures before the assurance requirement applies to them.

Under the draft timetable, companies with an average market capitalization of ¥3 trillion or more on the Prime Market are already subject to earlier sustainability disclosure requirements under a separate set of rules. For the new assurance framework, companies with market capitalization of at least ¥1 trillion but below ¥3 trillion are scheduled to enter the relevant phase from financial years beginning on or after April 1, 2028. Companies with market capitalization of at least ¥500 billion but below ¥1 trillion are scheduled to follow from financial years beginning on or after April 1, 2029.

The draft also provides transitional flexibility. In the initial years of applying the sustainability disclosure standards, companies may be able to use a two-stage reporting process. Instead of including all required information in the first annual securities report, they may submit the outstanding information later through a corrected filing, within the specified timetable. The arrangement is designed to give companies time to build reporting systems and collect information across their operations and supply chains.

For investors, the introduction of assurance is intended to improve confidence in sustainability information that is increasingly used in capital allocation, risk assessment and company comparisons. Environmental and social disclosures can influence investment decisions, but their usefulness depends on consistency, traceability and the quality of the underlying data. External assurance is intended to provide an additional level of scrutiny, although it does not remove the need for investors to assess the assumptions and methodologies behind individual disclosures.

A higher threshold for startup fundraising

The second part of the FSA package concerns startup fundraising. The draft raises the threshold below which certain securities offerings are exempt from filing a securities registration statement from less than ¥100 million to less than ¥500 million. The change is intended to reduce regulatory friction for qualifying fundraising activity and give startups more flexibility when seeking capital.

The increase does not mean that every private fundraising round below ¥500 million will automatically be free of regulatory obligations. The rules also address how amounts are aggregated and when securities notices and supporting documents must be made publicly available. Companies and intermediaries will still need to assess the structure of an offering, the type of securities involved and the applicable disclosure requirements.

The higher threshold could be particularly relevant to early-stage businesses that need to raise capital before they have the scale or reporting infrastructure of a public company. A lower administrative burden may make smaller offerings more practical, but investor protection remains an important consideration. The FSA’s draft therefore combines a higher exemption threshold with related provisions governing notices and calculations.

The proposals are open to public comment, with submissions due by December 13, 2026. The agency will consider feedback before finalizing the implementing rules. The planned commencement date is April 1, 2027.

The package reflects a two-sided policy approach. Japan is seeking to improve the quality of information available to investors in large listed companies while reducing some of the procedural barriers facing smaller businesses. The final impact will depend on the detailed rules adopted after consultation, the availability of qualified assurance providers and how effectively startups and their advisers interpret the revised fundraising framework.
