Bank of Korea Raises Rate to 3.25% as Chip Boom Lifts Growth but Inflation Risks Persist

South Korea’s rate hike reflects stronger chip exports, persistent inflation and mounting household debt risks for policymakers.

TTN FinanceNov 26, 2026 · 7:23 AM EST

The Bank of Korea raised its Base Rate by 25 basis points to 3.25% on November 26, ending a pause at its previous meeting and bringing borrowing costs back to a level last seen in late 2024. The decision was supported by five members of the Monetary Policy Board, while two preferred to leave the rate at 3.00%.

The move matters beyond South Korea. The country is a major supplier of memory chips and other components used in artificial-intelligence infrastructure. Strong demand for semiconductors has supported exports and investment, but the same expansion is contributing to a more complicated policy balance: growth is improving, while inflation remains above target and household borrowing is accelerating.

A stronger growth outlook changes the policy calculation

The central bank raised its 2026 growth forecast to 3.9%, from 3.3% in August, and its 2027 projection to 4.1%, from 2.9%. If achieved, next year’s pace would be the strongest since 2021. The Bank of Korea attributed the upgrade to robust exports and investment, particularly in the semiconductor sector, and expects the recovery to spread gradually into household consumption.

That forecast is significant because South Korea’s technology supply chain is closely tied to global AI capital spending. Demand for advanced memory and computing components has become an important source of industrial momentum. The central bank expects that infrastructure investment will continue to support exports, while the resulting income gains could eventually strengthen domestic demand.

But the growth picture is not uniformly strong. The bank said consumption had remained somewhat slow even as exports and investment accelerated. The outlook also depends on how long the semiconductor boom lasts and how effectively export income spreads through the wider economy. A concentration of growth in one globally exposed industry leaves the forecast vulnerable to a change in technology spending, trade restrictions or a downturn in external demand. Inflation remains the reason for tighter policy

Consumer-price inflation and core inflation both stood at 2.8% in October, according to the central bank’s statement. The Bank of Korea expects headline inflation to average 2.7% in 2026 and 2.4% in 2027. Its forecast for next year was raised from 2.3%, while core inflation is projected at 2.6% in both years.

The bank pointed to higher prices for personal services and durable goods, as well as the continuing pass-through of elevated costs. Energy prices have also become a concern as conflict in the Middle East disrupts supply chains and raises the risk of a prolonged oil-price shock. Stronger economic activity could add demand-side pressure before inflation has returned to the 2% target.

The decision therefore reflects a change in the balance of risks. In October, policymakers held rates at 3.00% while assessing the effects of earlier increases. By November, stronger growth and persistent price pressures made another increase more compelling. The two dissenting votes nevertheless show that the board was not unanimous about the need to tighten immediately.

Household debt limits the room for manoeuvre

Financial stability is the other major part of the policy equation. The central bank said household loan growth accelerated, with increases in both housing-related and other borrowing. Housing-price expectations remained elevated in Seoul and surrounding areas even though price growth had moderated in some locations.

Higher interest rates can restrain credit demand and discourage speculative borrowing, but they also raise debt-service costs for households with variable-rate loans or refinancing needs. South Korea’s large household-debt burden makes that transmission channel particularly important. A prolonged period of higher rates could weaken consumption even if it helps contain inflation.

What the decision means for global markets

The Bank of Korea’s action is a reminder that the AI investment cycle is influencing monetary policy through more than technology valuations. Semiconductor exports are supporting growth in a major Asian economy, while the energy and supply-chain environment is keeping inflation risks alive. The same combination can complicate decisions for other central banks that face stronger investment but persistent price pressures. Related context: How Fed Decisions Reach Stocks, Bonds and Crypto

For investors, the immediate signal is not that South Korea has committed to a long sequence of increases. The bank said future decisions would depend on inflation, economic activity and financial-stability conditions. Its statement leaves the timing of any further move open.

The key question for 2027 is whether the semiconductor-led expansion can sustain stronger growth without embedding higher inflation. If export momentum continues and consumer demand strengthens, the central bank may have less room to ease policy. If global AI spending cools or household borrowing weakens consumption, the growth forecast could prove harder to achieve.

South Korea has therefore entered a more delicate phase: a stronger economy has given policymakers a reason to raise rates, but the sources of that strength also expose the country to global technology and energy shocks. The November decision is best understood as an attempt to keep inflation expectations anchored while the export boom is still providing support.
