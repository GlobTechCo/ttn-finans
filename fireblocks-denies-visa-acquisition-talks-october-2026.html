Fireblocks Denies Visa Takeover Talks After Report of a $2.5 Billion Deal

Fireblocks rejected a report about acquisition talks with Visa. The dispute comes as payment networks expand their stablecoin infrastructure.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-22

Fireblocks denied on October 22 that it was in acquisition talks with Visa, pushing back against a report that placed a possible takeover at about $2.5 billion. The denial leaves the transaction unconfirmed and puts the focus on the distinction between an anonymously sourced deal report and the companies’ public responses.

Axios reported possible $2.5 billion acquisition talks, citing multiple sources. Fireblocks subsequently told The Block the report was news to the company and was not true at that time. A Visa spokesperson declined to discuss rumors or speculation. Neither response supports presenting a signed agreement, an agreed valuation or a completed acquisition as established fact.

The business behind the report

Fireblocks supplies technology for institutions to manage and move digital assets, including wallet and stablecoin infrastructure. That places it in a different part of the industry from a token issuer or an exchange primarily serving retail traders. The infrastructure business concerns how institutions authorize, secure and process transfers, rather than simply which cryptocurrency customers choose to buy.

Its January 2022 financing announcement documented a $550 million Series E round at an $8 billion valuation. That historical financing mark is a verified company disclosure. The price appearing in today’s disputed takeover report has a different status. The two should not be combined into a claim that Fireblocks has now accepted a specific reduction in value.

A private fundraising valuation also describes the terms of an investment at a particular moment. An acquisition can involve a different ownership scope, payment structure and set of obligations. Without agreed transaction documents, a comparison cannot establish what existing investors would receive. Our guide to mergers and acquisitions explains why headline consideration and the economics for shareholders can differ.

Visa’s stablecoin activity is already documented

Separate from the disputed acquisition story, Visa has published concrete information about its stablecoin operations. In a September 8 article, it reported more than 160 stablecoin-linked card programs in its fiscal second quarter and said stablecoin settlement volume had recently exceeded a $20 billion annualized run rate. Those figures are company-reported operating measures.

An annualized run rate extrapolates activity at a particular pace; it is not the same as a full year of recorded transactions. Settlement volume is also not Visa revenue. Keeping the units clear matters when comparing the scale of payment flows with the price of a company or the income a service earns from processing those flows.

Visa’s article describes a less visible constraint on new card programs: funding daily settlement before money is collected from cardholders. Some stablecoin-linked programs need that financing across weekends and holidays. This makes working capital, reliable transfer infrastructure and repayment controls commercially important even when the customer sees an ordinary card purchase.

The company describes a Credit Coop financing arrangement that uses settlement receivables and program data. That is an example of infrastructure and credit being combined to support payments. It is not evidence of an acquisition relationship with Fireblocks. Readers can distinguish the payment token from these operational arrangements through our stablecoin explainer.

Partnership, ownership and custody are separate questions

Payment businesses can expand through internal development, partnerships or acquisitions. A documented effort to increase stablecoin use does not prove a particular takeover rumor. Each route involves different integration costs and control over technology, and each requires evidence specific to the arrangement being discussed.

There is also a difference between software used to secure assets and the legal responsibility to safeguard customer funds. That distinction is relevant to our coverage of Coinbase Ireland’s safeguarding settlement: technical transfer capability and regulatory obligations address different risks. Neither disappears merely because a larger payments company becomes involved in the sector.

For now, the most important development is the public contradiction of the original report. A subsequent announcement, filing or clearly documented change in the companies’ positions would provide a new basis for assessment. Visa trades under V on the New York Stock Exchange; today’s reporting should not be read as confirmation that it has agreed to buy Fireblocks.
