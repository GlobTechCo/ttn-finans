Staffing Firm Randstad Reports Faster Growth as US Industrial Hiring Strengthens

Third-quarter results show stronger demand in North America and Germany, while weaker professional recruitment keeps the recovery uneven.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-21

Randstad, the Dutch recruitment and temporary-staffing group, reported faster third-quarter revenue growth Wednesday as demand for industrial workers strengthened in the United States and its German business continued recovering. The results offer a company-level sign of improving hiring appetite, although demand for technology and professional roles remains weaker.

Organic revenue per working day increased 3.9% from a year earlier, accelerating from 1.9% in the second quarter. Revenue totaled €6.115 billion, while underlying earnings before interest, taxes and amortization reached €211 million. Both the growth rate and underlying earnings exceeded analyst expectations reported by Reuters.

Industrial staffing leads the improvement

North American organic revenue rose 9%, including 10% growth across Randstad’s combined U.S. businesses. Within the United States, operational staffing, which includes industrial and other blue-collar assignments, grew 19%. Professional staffing increased 3%, while digital staffing was flat. Those differences matter more for assessing the labor market than the regional average alone.

A manufacturer adding shifts can create demand for temporary workers before it commits to permanent recruitment. Conversely, a business uncertain about future orders can reduce outside staffing without immediately changing its permanent workforce. That makes staffing orders useful evidence about employer behavior, while leaving them sensitive to individual client contracts and the recruiter’s market share.

Randstad’s results cannot establish that employment is improving across the entire U.S. economy. They measure revenue at one supplier, with its own mix of industries and customers. A broader assessment still requires payrolls, unemployment and participation data. Our guide to reading the U.S. jobs report explains why those measures can send different signals.

European demand remains mixed

Germany’s organic revenue grew 5%, and the Netherlands returned to growth with a 3% increase. France declined 3%, while Belgium and Luxembourg fell 5%. The geographical split argues against treating the quarter as evidence of a synchronized European hiring boom. Improvements in several markets coexist with continued retrenchment elsewhere.

The specialization figures reinforce that caution. Across the group, operational staffing grew 6%, but professional revenue slipped 1% and digital revenue fell 2%. Permanent-placement fees declined 4%. An employer’s willingness to purchase additional temporary labor therefore should not be confused with a broad recovery in permanent hiring or white-collar recruitment.

For investors, the distinction affects the quality of growth. Different staffing services generate different fee structures and margins. Faster sales in one category can offset weaker activity elsewhere without returning the overall business to its earlier profitability. Comparing the revenue mix with the margin trend is more informative than assuming that every additional euro of sales earns the same return.

Profit improves despite pressure on gross margin

Randstad’s underlying EBITA margin rose to 3.5% from 3.3% a year earlier, even as its underlying gross margin fell to 17.8% from 18.4%. Underlying operating expenses were €878 million, unchanged in reported terms. Keeping those expenses stable allowed a greater share of the additional gross profit to reach operating earnings.

The measures require care. Underlying EBITA removes specified integration costs and one-off items and is different from both operating profit and net income. The financial statements report operating profit of €177 million and net income of €110 million. Readers comparing the business with another recruiter should check that both companies’ profit measures make comparable adjustments.

Reuters reported that the shares were up about 20% in Amsterdam at 09:15 GMT, an intraday observation rather than a closing return. Such a reaction reflects how results compare with expectations as well as the absolute strength of the business. A sharp rally alone does not establish that future earnings growth is assured.

Management said activity trends improved into October. The next test is whether stronger assignments translate into sustained gross profit and cash generation as the quarter progresses. Continued recovery in industrial staffing would support that case; persistent weakness in professional recruitment would limit how broadly investors can read the improvement.
