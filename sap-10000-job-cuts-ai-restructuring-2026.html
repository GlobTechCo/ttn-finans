SAP Weighs 10,000 Job Cuts as AI Restructuring Reshapes Its Software Business

Reported restructuring would reshape Europe’s software leader as it embeds AI across products, operations and customer relationships.

TTN Research DeskNov 27, 2026 · 5:52 AM EST

SAP is preparing a substantial workforce reduction as it reshapes its software business for the artificial-intelligence era, Reuters reported on November 27, while Bloomberg has put the potential scale at at least 10,000 jobs. The German company has not announced a final programme. Its own response has been that it is reviewing how its organisation should evolve, but no further decisions on job cuts have been made.

The reports need to be separated carefully. Bloomberg first reported on November 26 that SAP was considering at least 10,000 job cuts, citing people familiar with the discussions. Reuters followed the next day with its own report that SAP was preparing to cut a sizeable number of jobs, based on two sources. Reuters said SAP’s works council had not yet been informed about the plans. The 10,000 figure remains a reported estimate, not a confirmed target, board decision or redundancy notice.

SAP’s response also matters. A company spokesperson told Dow Jones that SAP continuously reviews its organisation and workforce as artificial intelligence changes the business environment, but that no decisions had been made on restructuring or job cuts. The spokesperson said the company did not comment on speculation. That statement does not rule out a future programme, but it prevents investors from treating the reported number or timetable as settled fact.

If the reported figure is eventually adopted, 10,000 positions would equal about 8.8% of SAP’s 113,076 full-time equivalents at September 30, 2026, up from 110,730 a year earlier, according to the company’s quarterly statement. Bloomberg described the potential cuts as at least 9% of the workforce, a rounded estimate. SAP’s previous major restructuring was announced in January 2024 and initially affected about 8,000 positions, most expected to be covered by voluntary leave and internal reskilling. The company later expanded that programme, while hiring in growth areas offset reductions elsewhere. The comparison is useful, but it does not establish that the new discussions will use the same mechanisms.

The reports come against a strong financial backdrop. SAP generated third-quarter revenue of €10.27 billion, up 13% year on year. Cloud revenue increased 26% to €6.67 billion, and its current cloud backlog reached €24.14 billion, 28% higher than a year earlier. Cloud ERP Suite revenue rose 29%. These figures show that demand for SAP’s cloud products was growing; they do not prove that every part of the company is efficient or that the reported workforce review is unrelated to operating costs.

The strategic issue is how SAP converts its installed base into recurring cloud revenue while making artificial intelligence commercially useful. Its enterprise resource-planning software is embedded in customers’ finance, procurement, human-resources and supply-chain operations. Replacing those systems is difficult, which gives SAP a durable position. But the company must persuade customers that AI tools such as Joule and its broader Business AI offering improve business processes enough to justify additional spending. The infrastructure boom behind that transition is also changing the economics of the sector, as shown by the investment and backlog pressures facing suppliers such as Dell in the AI-server market.

AI can affect SAP in two directions. Automation may reduce the time and labour required for software development, customer support and internal administration. At the same time, generative and agentic systems could make some software functions easier to replicate, increasing pressure on established vendors to defend pricing and demonstrate the value of integrated platforms. That is a strategic explanation for restructuring discussions, not evidence that AI has already replaced 10,000 SAP employees. Reuters has also reported that SAP has been replacing some expensive AI models used in its products with cheaper alternatives, a sign that management is examining the cost of delivering AI features as well as their potential revenue.

For investors, a smaller cost base could improve margins if revenue continues to grow. The counterargument is that a large restructuring may require severance and other one-off charges, disrupt product teams and weaken customer relationships if experienced engineers, consultants or sales staff leave. SAP’s cloud backlog is not the same as revenue already recognised: the company still has to deliver projects, support customers and convert contracted demand into sales. Cuts that impair execution could undermine some of the growth the restructuring is intended to protect. The broader question of whether AI spending produces durable returns is examined in TTN Finance’s analysis of the financing behind the AI boom.

The labour process will be particularly important in Germany, where employee representatives and works councils have a formal role in workplace consultation. Reuters reported that SAP’s works council had not yet been informed, suggesting the discussions had not reached the stage of a completed employee process. The eventual scope, timing and mix of voluntary departures, retraining and compulsory redundancies could therefore differ materially from the initial report. Investors should not model savings or restructuring charges before the company provides those details.

The next evidence will be a formal SAP announcement, disclosure of the affected business areas and any consultation timetable. Until then, the reliable picture is narrower: SAP is growing cloud revenue, investing in AI and reviewing its organisation, while reports of at least 10,000 job cuts remain unconfirmed. The financial significance will depend less on the headline number than on whether SAP can lower its cost base without weakening the products and customer relationships that support its cloud backlog.
