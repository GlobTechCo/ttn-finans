SAP Weighs 10,000 Job Cuts as AI Restructuring Reshapes Its Software Business

Reported restructuring would reshape Europe’s software leader as it embeds AI across products, operations and customer relationships.

TTN Research DeskNov 27, 2026 · 5:52 AM EST

SAP is preparing a major workforce restructuring as it tries to reshape its software business around artificial intelligence, with reports pointing to at least 10,000 potential job cuts. The company has not confirmed the proposed number, leaving investors to weigh a reported cost-reduction plan against strong cloud growth.

Reuters reported on November 27 that the German business-software group was preparing to eliminate a sizeable number of positions, citing two people familiar with the matter. The report followed Bloomberg’s account the previous evening that SAP was considering at least 10,000 job cuts as part of an AI-driven restructuring. Reuters said it could not independently verify the scale or details, and SAP had not confirmed the plan. The 10,000 figure should therefore be treated as reported, not as an announced target or a completed decision.

If the Bloomberg figure is accurate, the proposed reduction would affect at least 9% of SAP’s global workforce. The company reported 113,076 employees at the end of September in its third-quarter statement. That makes the potential restructuring unusually large for a company whose recent financial performance has been expanding: SAP reported third-quarter revenue of €10.27 billion, up 13% year on year, while cloud revenue increased 26% to €6.67 billion. Its current cloud backlog reached €24.1 billion, up 28%. Related context: Dell's $132.3B AI Backlog Tests the Server Boom Related context: The AI Boom’s Next Constraint May Be Its Balance Sheet

The contrast is central to the story. SAP is not describing a business in immediate revenue collapse. It is a company moving customers from traditional software licences and maintenance toward cloud subscriptions, while investing in AI features that can be embedded in finance, procurement, human resources and supply-chain applications. Management has presented its “Autonomous Enterprise” strategy as a way to make its software more valuable by adding AI assistance and automation to business processes. The reported restructuring suggests that the shift may also change the organisation required to build, sell and support those products.

The reported plan is not simply a conventional cost-cutting exercise. Bloomberg’s account, relayed by Reuters and other outlets, described an effort to reorganise the company for the AI era, with departments asked to identify potential reductions and product development, engineering and sales among the areas under review. SAP’s public response has been more limited: it said it continuously reviews its organisation to keep it aligned with strategy and customer needs, while declining to confirm whether cuts are planned. Without a formal announcement, it is not possible to determine which roles, countries or business units would be affected, how much of the reduction might come through voluntary departures, or what restructuring charges could result.

The strategic pressure is understandable. Enterprise software companies face a two-sided AI challenge. They can use generative and agentic systems to automate tasks inside their own operations, potentially lowering the cost of development, customer support and administration. At the same time, AI tools could make some traditional software functions easier to reproduce, forcing vendors to defend their pricing and demonstrate that their platforms remain essential. SAP’s advantage is its deep integration into customers’ core systems and business data. Replacing an enterprise resource planning platform is difficult because finance, purchasing, payroll, logistics and compliance processes are interconnected. But that installed base does not remove the need to deliver useful AI products at a cost customers will accept.

The company’s latest results provide an important counterpoint to the restructuring reports. SAP said cloud ERP Suite revenue rose 29% in the third quarter, while its current cloud backlog increased 28%. It also raised its 2026 cloud revenue outlook toward the upper end of a €25.8 billion to €26.2 billion range at constant currencies. Those figures indicate continued demand for its cloud transition. They do not prove that every part of the organisation is efficient, nor do they establish that AI has already replaced the work of thousands of employees. The connection between the reported job cuts and AI should therefore be understood as a strategic rationale attributed to people familiar with the discussions, not evidence that automation has directly displaced a specific number of workers.

For investors, the potential financial effects run in both directions. A smaller cost base could support operating margins and cash generation over time, especially if revenue continues to grow. But a restructuring of this size could require significant one-off expenses, create execution risks and unsettle staff responsible for product development and customer relationships. Reducing engineering capacity too aggressively could weaken the innovation pipeline; cuts in sales and service could also make it harder to convert the cloud backlog into recognised revenue. The quality and timing of any savings matter as much as the headline number.

The labour dimension is particularly important in Germany. Negotiations with employee representatives can influence the pace, form and cost of workforce changes. A reported plan is not equivalent to a final redundancy programme, and the ultimate number may change after consultation. Investors should wait for details on the timetable, affected functions, severance or retraining arrangements and expected charges before modelling the financial impact.

SAP’s next task is to show that its AI-first strategy can produce durable customer demand and better economics without weakening the enterprise relationships that underpin its business. The reported 10,000-job figure has attracted attention because it would represent a substantial organisational shift. Until SAP confirms a plan, however, the central facts remain its strong cloud growth, its stated AI ambitions and an unverified restructuring report.
