China Proposes AI Export Controls While Keeping Model Services Accessible

A draft covers 23 AI technology categories, while officials distinguish transfers of restricted technology from overseas access through apps and APIs. Temporary registration requirements start immediately.

TTN Research DeskOct 16, 2026 · 1:08 PM EDT

China proposed new export restrictions covering 23 categories of artificial-intelligence technology on Friday, while setting out a distinction between transferring restricted technology abroad and letting overseas customers use a service. The draft is part of a wider review covering 40 technologies across AI, biotechnology and solar manufacturing.

The commerce and science ministries are accepting comments through November 15. Alongside the draft, they introduced temporary pre-export registration for specified technology transfers, including relevant transactions already underway. The consultation and the registration requirement therefore have different timing: the proposed catalogue is not yet final, while registration begins with the announcement.

Access to a model is different from transferring it

The commerce ministry’s explanation says overseas deployment involving restricted technology would fall within the proposed controls. Providing access through APIs, apps, websites and software-as-a-service would sit outside the large-model technology export framework described in the proposal. It also sets out facilitated treatment for certain activities and excludes technology already in the public domain through open-source release or academic publication.

For a customer, those distinctions can produce very different purchasing choices. Calling a hosted service means sending a request to a provider-operated system. Acquiring technology for a locally operated deployment gives the customer a different degree of possession and control. A business cannot assume that permission for one arrangement establishes permission for the other.

A sourcing issue for AI buyers

The practical question for an overseas buyer is which part of its planned arrangement constitutes a transfer. A subscription, a technology licence and an acquisition of intellectual property are different transactions. Contracts that appear similar commercially can require different treatment when the underlying technology and deployment model differ.

The draft should not be read as a blanket withdrawal of Chinese AI services from international markets. Nor does continued service availability establish that every form of local deployment will remain equally accessible. The final wording and its application will determine that boundary.

Why the distinction matters beyond China

TTN’s coverage of Germany’s planned industrial AI agreement concerns another part of the same procurement problem: where computing is operated, who supplies it and what a customer actually receives. Access to capacity and ownership of technology are separate commercial assets.

For companies planning multi-year AI projects, the draft adds a reason to document deployment assumptions before signing. An architecture built around a hosted service may have different operational dependencies from one built around technology installed locally. Friday’s announcement brings that distinction into policy, with the consultation still open and no basis yet for treating the proposed catalogue as final.
