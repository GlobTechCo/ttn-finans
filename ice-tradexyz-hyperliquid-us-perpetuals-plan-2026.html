NYSE Owner ICE Plans a US Market for Hyperliquid-Based Perpetual Futures

ICE is backing a plan to bring Hyperliquid-based derivatives to US retail traders. The agreement is signed, but trading still needs regulatory approvals.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-29

Intercontinental Exchange, the owner of the New York Stock Exchange, agreed Thursday to work with Trade[XYZ] on bringing Hyperliquid-based perpetual futures into a U.S.-regulated market. The agreement is a new route toward domestic access to a product widely associated with offshore crypto trading, rather than a launch that Americans can use immediately.

ICE plans to operate a dedicated market and clearing structure and is taking a minority investment in Trade[XYZ]. Regulatory approvals remain necessary, including those supporting leveraged trading. The companies describe a retail-focused business separate from ICE’s established institutional futures operation. They did not announce a customer trading start date.

That separation is central to the proposal. The commercial opportunity is to connect continuous, blockchain-based derivatives markets with regulated operators and brokers. It is not simply a plan to place a crypto-trading website under the New York Stock Exchange name or give retail customers access to every existing Hyperliquid contract.

The contracts and the infrastructure do different jobs

Trade[XYZ] supplies expertise in designing markets that reference assets such as equities, indices, commodities and currencies. Hyperliquid supplies the underlying technology. ICE would provide regulated market operation and clearing. The planned distribution includes participating futures brokers and ICE’s venture with OKX, whose own relevant U.S. approvals are also pending.

Hyperliquid’s documentation for builder-deployed perpetuals, known as HIP-3, helps explain the distinction between infrastructure and operator. A deployer defines the market, including its price source and contract specifications, and manages settings such as leverage limits. Using a common trading engine does not make every deployer’s products identical or transfer every operating responsibility to the blockchain.

The protocol documentation also describes independent margining and order books for each deployed exchange. That is useful technical context, rather than proof that ICE’s proposed legal and clearing arrangements are already in place. The final U.S. product would have to satisfy the requirements applicable to its operator, contracts and customer access.

No expiry does not mean no carrying cost

A perpetual futures contract has no fixed expiration date. Instead of settling on a scheduled maturity, it uses a funding mechanism to help keep its price close to the asset it references. Holding the contract creates price exposure; it does not by itself give the holder ownership of the underlying share, commodity or currency.

Hyperliquid’s current funding documentation describes payments between the long and short sides, with direction influenced by the relationship between the contract price and its reference price. Funding is paid hourly on its existing markets. Those are current protocol mechanics, not a confirmed specification for every contract in the proposed ICE venue.

This distinction affects the economics of keeping a position open. A trader can face changing funding payments as well as price movements. When leverage is involved, losses also consume collateral more quickly. TTN’s explanation of liquidation and margin describes why a position can be closed before an investor has lost the entire value of the underlying asset.

US policy provides a path, not blanket permission

The Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s policy statement adopted May 29 and published June 3 accompanied an order allowing a perpetual contract referencing bitcoin’s spot price to be listed as a futures contract. For asset classes outside the scope of that order, the agency said case-by-case review under Regulation 40.3 was appropriate.

That context matters for a proposal spanning crypto and traditional financial assets. Earlier regulatory action on one class of contract should not be read as automatic approval for every equity, commodity or currency perpetual. Thursday’s announcement itself preserves the condition that the new arrangements require permission before customers can access them.

The ICE agreement is also distinct from the earlier Payward and Kraken-related Hyperliquid plan, which involved different operating entities and a test-network milestone. Multiple firms can pursue regulated deployment of similar infrastructure without sharing one approval or launch schedule.

For investors assessing the news, the next meaningful evidence will be regulatory filings, permissions, published contract terms and functioning broker access. The signed commercial agreement establishes the partners’ intent. Whether that becomes a significant U.S. retail market will depend on those operating milestones and on customers actually choosing to trade there.
