Abu Dhabi Court Orders Crypto Firms to Keep Binance Heist Assets Frozen

The order covers 18 companies and billions in stolen tokens, turning asset recovery into a cross-border legal test.

TTN Research DeskNov 19, 2026 · 6:15 AM EST

A court order adds legal force to asset freezes

An Abu Dhabi Global Market court has ordered 18 cryptocurrency-related companies to maintain freezes on digital assets linked to the October 29 theft of $6.3 billion from wallets operated by Binance. The order is designed to prevent the tokens from being moved while proceedings continue and gives the exchange a legal route to pursue assets already identified by industry participants.

Reuters reported that the companies named in the order include Circle, the issuer of USDC, and World Liberty Financial, which issues the USD1 stablecoin. The court said the named companies were not accused of wrongdoing. Their role is connected to assets that passed through or were held by their systems.

The order also restricts the affected firms from lending, swapping or mixing the relevant tokens with other assets. A freeze can preserve a traceable balance, but it does not by itself return the property to its rightful owner or establish the final legal ownership of every token.

Billions have been intercepted, but recovery is not complete

Binance has previously said about $2.85 billion of the stolen assets had been frozen or intercepted by third parties. The figure includes tokens held by Circle and World Liberty Financial. Tether, issuer of USDT, has separately said it froze about $1.5 billion in assets linked to the incident, although it was not included among the 18 companies named in the court order. Tether said it was working directly with Binance on the return of frozen assets.

Those figures should not be added together without qualification. They describe amounts reported by different participants and may overlap or change as assets move and market prices fluctuate. The amount frozen is therefore not necessarily the same as the amount ultimately recovered in a form that can be returned.

Binance has said it absorbed the financial impact of the stolen funds and reimbursed customers. That does not end the recovery effort. It has retained U.S. law firm Gibson Dunn to support the legal process.

Why the order matters to stablecoin issuers and exchanges

Centralized stablecoin issuers and exchanges can sometimes identify addresses and restrict assets within systems they control. That ability can help preserve funds after a theft, but it also places companies in a difficult position when a legal order crosses jurisdictions or involves disputed claims. Firms must assess whether the instruction is valid, which assets it covers and how to comply without freezing unrelated customer property.

The case illustrates a distinction explored in TTN’s guide to DeFi risks: a blockchain transaction, a token issuer and an application are separate layers, each with its own permissions and failure points. A freeze imposed by an issuer does not mean the underlying blockchain has reversed a transaction.

The role of Abu Dhabi’s financial jurisdiction

Abu Dhabi Global Market is the financial free zone in which Binance has established a major global hub. Binance has described the Financial Services Regulatory Authority there as its primary regulator. The court process gives the exchange access to a legal jurisdiction connected to its operations.

The Financial Services Regulatory Authority said on October 30 that it was aware of unauthorized withdrawals involving Binance and was engaging with the exchange while monitoring the incident. That statement confirms regulatory engagement, but it is separate from the later court order and should not be mistaken for a finding about who carried out the attack.

U.S. authorities have attributed the October 29 incident to North Korea, according to Reuters. The court’s asset-freezing process has a narrower purpose: preserving property and supporting recovery. A freeze is not a criminal conviction, and the named companies are not accused of causing the breach.

A December hearing will test the next stage

A further hearing is scheduled for December 1, when the court is expected to consider whether the proprietary injunction should continue and on what terms. The outcome should not be assumed in advance.

The case also raises practical questions about the evidence needed to connect particular tokens to the theft and what happens when assets move through decentralized protocols or services outside the court’s direct jurisdiction. The order can strengthen cooperation, but it cannot guarantee that every token will be located or recovered.

Investors and users should avoid treating the headline dollar value as a measure of the final customer loss. Binance says it has made customers whole, while the recovery effort concerns assets taken from the exchange’s wallets. The distinction between customer reimbursement, tokens frozen and assets ultimately returned is essential to understanding the financial consequences.

What the case says about crypto custody

The incident demonstrates that custody risk extends beyond the security of a single wallet. Exchanges, custodians, token issuers, blockchain analytics firms, law enforcement agencies and courts can all become involved after a large theft, and coordination can determine whether assets remain traceable long enough for recovery.

Users assessing a platform should understand how assets are held, what security controls protect signing systems and what recourse exists after unauthorized withdrawals. The TTN explainer on Bitcoin mining economics covers a different part of the crypto system, but it reinforces a broader point: network-level mechanics and the business practices of service providers should not be confused.

The Abu Dhabi order is an important step in the recovery process, not a final resolution. The December hearing, further disclosures from Binance and the companies involved, and any verified returns of assets will determine how much of the stolen value can ultimately be recovered.
