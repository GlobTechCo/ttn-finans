U.S. Inflation Hits 0.4% in October, Sending Treasury Yields Higher

Headline and core inflation both exceeded forecasts, lifting Treasury yields and hitting stocks after an earlier rally on falling crude prices.

TTN Research DeskNov 10, 2026

A hotter-than-expected U.S. inflation report sent Treasury yields higher on Tuesday and complicated the Federal Reserve's December rate decision. Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October from September, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, against the 0.3% increase forecast in a Reuters poll. The core index, which excludes food and energy, climbed 0.3% rather than the expected 0.2%.

The surprise interrupted a rally fueled earlier by falling oil prices. Bonds sold off, the dollar strengthened and stocks lost ground as traders priced in more monetary tightening. Those market moves reflect changing expectations, not a decision already taken by the Fed.

There is an unusual gap in the report: the BLS could not publish the normal annual inflation comparison because a 2025 federal funding lapse prevented October 2025 data collection. The widely quoted 0.4% figure measures one month's price change, not the preceding twelve months.

What the official report shows

Headline CPI slowed from a 0.6% increase in September to 0.4% in October. That is a moderation in the month-to-month rate of increase, but the result still surpassed expectations. The core measure increased 0.3% for a third straight month, suggesting that the surprise was not limited to gasoline or other volatile components.

Shelter costs rose 0.3%, with the BLS saying the category accounted for more than thirty percent of the headline month's increase. Housing-related expenses are significant because they represent a large share of household spending and often adjust more slowly than traded commodity prices.

Food prices increased 0.5% after being unchanged in September. The food-at-home component, broadly associated with groceries, rose 0.4%, while food away from home increased 0.5%. Those categories have different implications for individual households, but both illustrate the persistence of pressure on everyday budgets.

The energy index rose 0.7%, much less than September's 5.2% jump. Gasoline prices increased 1.0% after seasonal adjustment, and electricity prices rose 0.9%. Natural gas prices declined 0.8%.

Why annual inflation is not available

Without an October 2025 baseline, ordinary year-over-year comparisons for many CPI series are unavailable. Any independently estimated annual figure should be labeled as an estimate, not presented as an official BLS reading. That matters when investors compare this report with periods in which both monthly and annual rates were published.

Treasury yields respond

In Tuesday's trading, Reuters reported the two-year Treasury yield rising by about 19 basis points to roughly 4.74%; the ten-year yield approached 5%. The figures are intraday levels, not closing prices. Short-dated yields react especially quickly to changes in expectations for Federal Reserve policy.

The two-year yield is particularly sensitive to the expected path of short-term interest rates. If investors anticipate that the Federal Reserve will keep its policy rate higher for longer, or raise it by more than previously assumed, that expectation can lift shorter-dated Treasury yields quickly.

Higher yields also influence equity markets. When government bonds offer a greater return, the relative appeal of shares can change. Financing costs may rise for companies and households, while the current value of future corporate earnings can fall under higher discount rates.

In afternoon market coverage, Reuters reported declines in major U.S. stock indexes and gold, with the dollar advancing. Such changes reflect market responses to new information and may reverse during later trading. They are not evidence that a single CPI release determines the direction of markets for weeks ahead.

The Federal Reserve faces a difficult balance

The inflation surprise intensified debate over the Fed's December policy decision. Reuters said markets had increased the probability assigned to a larger rate increase. That probability is an estimate derived from market pricing; it is not an official policy decision or a promise by Fed officials.

That reversal is clearest against the morning's backdrop. Earlier on November 10, traders welcomed lower Brent crude and gains in Asian and European equities. Our pre-release analysis of Brent below $90 captured the earlier market mood. The CPI release then changed the interest-rate calculation.

What households can take from the figures

The CPI measures changes in prices across a broad basket of goods and services. It is not a personalized measure of the cost of living for every household. People face different bills depending on whether they rent, own a home, drive, use public transportation or spend more on medical care.

Some categories offered relief. Used vehicle prices declined 1.2%, and communications prices fell slightly. Those reductions help offset rising categories in the overall index but may mean little to a household that is not buying a car or changing a phone plan.

What to watch next

Investors will evaluate subsequent labor, spending and price data for evidence that the October surprise represents a durable inflation problem or a temporary deviation. The next CPI report, covering November, is scheduled for December 10.

Our pre-release report on Asian stocks showed how lower oil prices influenced sentiment before the CPI data arrived. Tuesday's new data demonstrate why those expectations can remain fragile until the official numbers are available.

The immediate story is an inflation surprise and a rapid repricing of interest-rate expectations. The next evidence will come from incoming employment and price reports, with November CPI due on December 10. Whether October marks a persistent acceleration cannot be determined from one monthly release.
