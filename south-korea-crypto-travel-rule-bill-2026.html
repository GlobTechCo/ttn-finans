South Korea Bill Would Put Crypto Travel Rule Duties Directly Into Law

A South Korean bill would clarify crypto transfer-data rules and require risk checks before providers begin new relationships with counterparties.

TTN Research DeskNov 16, 2026

South Korea’s National Assembly has received a bill that would put clearer statutory rules around information accompanying virtual-asset transfers and require crypto service providers to assess new business relationships for money-laundering risk. The proposal, filed on November 13 and reported by Korean media on November 16, is aimed at strengthening the country’s anti-money-laundering framework as digital-asset transactions become more complex.

The amendment was introduced by Democratic Party lawmaker Kim Hyun-jung with 11 other legislators. It would revise the Act on Reporting and Use of Certain Financial Transaction Information, South Korea’s main anti-money-laundering statute for financial institutions and virtual-asset service providers. The bill is at the receipt stage. It has not passed committee review, been approved by the National Assembly or become law.

The distinction matters because South Korea already has a Travel Rule regime. The existing framework requires virtual-asset service providers to transmit information about the customers sending and receiving assets when transfers take place between regulated providers. The information includes customer names and wallet addresses, with additional identifying information available when requested by the relevant authority or receiving provider.

What the Current Travel Rule Requires

The rules have also changed recently. An amendment to the Enforcement Decree took effect on August 20, 2026, removing the former 1 million won threshold for information-sharing between virtual-asset service providers. Under the updated provision, the information requirement applies to transfers between providers regardless of the transferred amount. That means the proposed bill is not the first introduction of a Travel Rule in South Korea, nor does it simply extend the rule from large transfers to smaller ones. That threshold change is already in force.

A transfer between regulated platforms is therefore not treated as anonymous merely because it is recorded on a public blockchain. The sending provider passes customer and address information to the receiving provider, helping compliance teams connect on-chain activity with the customers involved.

The proposed amendment would instead place clearer standards for providing and processing transfer information into the statute itself. Its focus on identifying and managing illicit flows also overlaps with tools such as TRM Labs’ new law-enforcement crypto-tracing service, although the bill concerns provider obligations rather than a tracing product. The bill’s statement of reasons also calls for stronger requirements for virtual-asset service providers and new risk-assessment and verification systems before a provider starts dealing with another virtual-asset provider or with a counterparty that is not itself a virtual-asset provider.

Why New-Counterparty Checks Matter

The second element could change how exchanges assess business partners. Before entering a relationship with another exchange, payment company or other service provider, a platform may need to establish the counterparty’s identity, business activity and financial-crime risks. The bill seeks to make those checks systematic.

The proposal also addresses self-hosted wallets. Newsis reported that exchanges would have to check the destination address and identify its owner when a customer transfers assets to a wallet they control. This is a verification requirement, not a ban on self-hosted wallets or a prohibition on all withdrawals. The final proof standards would depend on the law and later guidance.

For exchanges, the framework could make onboarding and monitoring more demanding. Newsis also reported that the bill would keep exchanges ultimately responsible for customer due diligence even when they outsource verification, require reports to the Financial Intelligence Unit about certain significant changes involving a representative or major shareholder, and allow administrative fines of up to 100 million won. These would be proposed duties and penalties, not current requirements.

What the Bill Does Not Yet Change

The proposal remains a legislative initiative, not a new operating rule. The current Travel Rule requirements continue to apply under the existing Act and Enforcement Decree. The bill’s language may be revised during parliamentary scrutiny, and the final obligations will depend on the provisions lawmakers approve and any regulations or guidance issued afterward.

For customers, the proposal does not itself create a new fee, a blanket withdrawal delay or a general ban on self-hosted wallets. Any such change would need to appear in enacted provisions or official implementation guidance.

South Korea’s approach also matters for foreign providers that serve Korean customers. They will need compatible data-sharing and privacy controls if the statutory duties are enacted. The proposal fits a broader regional move toward crypto reporting, including Hong Kong’s crypto-asset reporting law.

For now, the bill signals an effort to move parts of crypto anti-money-laundering compliance from detailed subordinate rules into clearer statutory duties, while making risk checks on new counterparties more explicit. The immediate legal position has not changed: the existing Travel Rule remains in force, including the removal of its former value threshold in August. The next meaningful milestone will be the bill’s committee review and any revisions made before a parliamentary vote.
