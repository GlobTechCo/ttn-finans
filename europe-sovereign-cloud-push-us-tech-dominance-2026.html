Europe's Sovereign Cloud Push Challenges US Tech Dominance, but Migration Remains Slow

European sovereignty rules are creating cloud opportunities, but migration costs and US hyperscaler scale limit rapid market-share shifts.

TTN Research DeskNov 23, 2026 · 4:35 AM EST

Europe's effort to reduce dependence on American cloud providers is moving from political debate into procurement decisions, creating new opportunities for regional technology companies while presenting a long-term challenge to Amazon, Microsoft and Google. Governments and regulated industries are increasingly evaluating cloud services according to who controls the infrastructure, encryption keys and operational decisions, rather than simply where customer data is physically stored.

A Reuters analysis published on November 23 found that digital sovereignty requirements are becoming more influential in European public-sector and regulated-industry contracts. The shift reflects concerns about geopolitical dependence, service disruptions and the possibility that foreign legal or political decisions could affect access to critical digital infrastructure.

European policymakers have begun translating those concerns into contracts. In April, the European Commission awarded four sovereign-cloud contracts worth up to €180 million over six years to groups led by Luxembourg's Post Telecom, Belgium's Proximus, Germany's StackIT and France's Scaleway. The Dutch government also signed a framework agreement with StackIT intended to reduce dependence on non-European suppliers.

For European cloud companies, this creates a potential source of demand that goes beyond competing on price. Providers able to demonstrate local operational control, regulatory compliance and reliable infrastructure can become credible alternatives for sensitive workloads. IONOS, StackIT, Scaleway, Proximus and other regional providers may benefit as government departments and financial institutions reassess their technology contracts.

However, the opportunity should not be confused with an immediate transfer of market leadership. Amazon Web Services, Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud still control more than 70% of Europe's cloud market. The European Commission's June draft of the proposed Cloud and AI Development Act cited a decline in the market share of EU cloud providers from 29% in 2017 to 15% in 2022. The figures illustrate how difficult it has been for European companies to compete with the scale and investment capacity of American hyperscalers.

The difference between political ambition and commercial reality is particularly visible in migration costs. Large organisations have spent years building applications around specific cloud platforms. Moving those systems can require software redevelopment, security reviews, employee training and extensive testing. A migration that looks attractive from a strategic perspective may be difficult to justify if it introduces operational disruption or higher costs.

IONOS executives told Reuters that public-sector procurement reviews and technical assessments can extend over 12 to 24 months. The company's public-sector strategist also said only a single-digit percentage of discussions with public-sector customers currently result in contracts. That suggests the addressable market is growing, but the conversion of interest into recurring revenue remains a gradual process.

Cloud sovereignty also does not necessarily require abandoning American technology. Google Cloud's S3NS joint venture with French defence and technology group Thales has obtained France's SecNumCloud certification for sensitive government and regulated-industry workloads. The arrangement demonstrates how American infrastructure and European operational partnerships can coexist.

The hyperscalers are adapting rather than ignoring the trend. Amazon Web Services said it tested the ability of its European Sovereign Cloud to operate independently of infrastructure outside the European Union. Microsoft has pointed to partner-operated projects including Bleu in France and Delos Cloud in Germany. Google is also emphasising operational autonomy, encryption-key control and cloud-exit provisions in procurement discussions.

The European regulatory environment reinforces this shift. The Digital Operational Resilience Act establishes technology-risk requirements for financial firms, while NIS2 strengthens cybersecurity obligations across critical sectors. The proposed Cloud and AI Development Act could add further support for European infrastructure investment, although the precise effects will depend on the final legislation and how procurement rules are implemented.

For investors, the opportunity is therefore distributed across several business models. European infrastructure operators could gain new public-sector contracts, cybersecurity companies may benefit from additional compliance spending, and software providers offering portable or self-hosted solutions could attract customers seeking to reduce dependence on a single platform.

Nextcloud, which develops software that organisations can host themselves as an alternative to conventional productivity and collaboration platforms, told Reuters that demand had increased roughly fivefold since the beginning of US President Donald Trump's second term. T-Systems also reported a substantial increase in demand for its sovereign-cloud offering. These are company-reported figures, not independently audited market-growth measurements, but they illustrate the commercial interest.

Nevertheless, European alternatives still face challenges involving scale, service breadth, investment requirements and international compatibility. Banks and multinational businesses may need infrastructure that can operate across several jurisdictions. Temenos, a Swiss banking software company, told Reuters that sovereign-cloud initiatives were not yet mature enough for many large banks with international operations.

The most likely outcome is selective migration rather than a wholesale replacement of American platforms. Governments and regulated industries may move sensitive workloads first while keeping established applications and less sensitive services with existing providers.

That distinction matters for AWS, Microsoft and Google. They could lose individual contracts or face higher compliance costs without losing their dominant positions in the wider cloud market. For European providers, the prize is not necessarily replacing the hyperscalers but securing a larger share of strategically important workloads.

The next indicators to watch are contract awards, migration volumes, infrastructure investment and the ability of European providers to deliver reliable services at competitive prices. Digital sovereignty is becoming a real commercial requirement, but turning that political priority into a profitable and scalable European cloud industry will take years.
