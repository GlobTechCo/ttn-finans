Hong Kong Dollar Stablecoin Targets Solana and Visa–Mastercard Settlement

Standard Chartered-backed Anchorpoint announced plans for HKDAP on Solana and talks on card settlement. The token remains in restricted beta access.

TTN Research Desk2026-10-30

Anchorpoint, the Standard Chartered-backed issuer of the Hong Kong dollar stablecoin HKDAP, announced plans Friday to expand onto Solana and explore settlement through Visa and Mastercard. The parallel initiatives aim to connect a regulated digital version of the currency with public blockchains and established payment networks, rather than confining it to a single distribution channel.

The announcements describe development and exploration, not a completed launch across those networks. Anchorpoint’s transparency page still identifies HKDAP as being in restricted beta access. That gap between a partnership announcement and an available service is central to understanding what changed Friday, and what customers still cannot assume is ready to use.

A Hong Kong dollar token, with an identified issuer

HKDAP is designed to represent HK$1 per token. The Hong Kong Monetary Authority’s register lists Anchorpoint Financial Limited as licensee FRS01, effective April 10, 2026. The regulator identifies Standard Chartered Bank (Hong Kong), HKT and Animoca Brands as the partners behind the issuer. Licensing identifies the accountable entity; it does not turn the token into a government-issued currency.

A stablecoin’s reference currency matters. A Hong Kong dollar token is not a US dollar token merely because both use the word dollar. A company with Hong Kong dollar receipts, expenses or assets may want settlement in that unit, while a business operating in another currency may still need conversion. Moving money onto a blockchain does not remove the need to match financial obligations with the currency being held.

The structure also differs from holding a speculative network token. HKDAP’s intended value comes from its relationship to reserves and redemption, rather than a promise that its price will rise. TTN’s guide to stablecoin backing and depeg risk explains why the asset pool, legal claim and conversion process matter alongside the stated peg.

Solana adds a proposed distribution route

Anchorpoint said its collaboration with the Solana Foundation would support planned native issuance and redemption on Solana, with retail payments and tokenized-asset trading among the intended uses. The foundation will also join its Tokenised Money Adoption Programme. Neither the collaboration announcement nor the issuer’s current access disclosures establish a general retail launch date for the new network.

Native issuance means creating the issuer’s token on the destination network, rather than simply assuming that an existing token can be moved there through an unrelated bridge. For potential users, the important checks include the supported chain, official contract and distributor. A familiar name or ticker alone does not establish that a token is the issuer’s genuine product.

The commercial opportunity is distribution. Wallets, applications and financial services can provide ways to reach users who would not approach an issuer directly. But software compatibility is only one step. Customer eligibility, access to banking, liquidity and the terms for converting back into conventional money all affect whether a technically transferable token becomes a practical payment instrument.

Card-network settlement is different from paying at every checkout

In a separate release, Anchorpoint announced partnerships with Mastercard and Visa to explore adding HKDAP to their supported stablecoins. The initial focus is settlement options for card issuers. That is a potential change in how participating financial institutions settle obligations, rather than confirmation that every merchant accepting either card network now accepts HKDAP directly.

The distinction is consequential for consumers. A card payment can look familiar at checkout while the funding or settlement arrangements behind it change. Conversely, a stablecoin integration can be commercially important without requiring the shopper to hold a token or use a new wallet. Friday’s releases do not establish the final customer experience, available markets or complete operating terms.

These initiatives sit alongside other efforts to join regulated money with existing distribution. HSBC’s RedCoin rollout through PayMe illustrates a different route to customers. The relevant comparison is how each arrangement handles access, conversion and actual use, rather than whether every announcement carries the same stablecoin label.

Redemption and reserve disclosures remain separate tests

Anchorpoint’s whitepaper says HKDAP reserves are held in trust for token holders, with a value required to cover outstanding tokens at par. It describes redemption through authorized distributors and an aim to process distributor requests within one business day. Blockchain transfer speed therefore should not be confused with an unconditional promise of immediate bank-account redemption for every holder.

The issuer currently describes its weekly reserve statements as unaudited and says periodic independent attestations will follow full launch. Those are different levels of disclosure. Its whitepaper also states that HKDAP is not legal tender or government insured. The new partnerships do not replace the need to examine those terms, and bank backing should not be interpreted as a government guarantee.

For the payments industry, Friday’s development is a plan to make regulated local-currency tokens useful across more systems. Evidence of execution would include a confirmed network launch, eligible distribution channels, working settlement arrangements and published operating terms. Until then, the significant change is the scope of the proposed connections, with broader customer access still to be delivered.
